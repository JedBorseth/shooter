# Art direction

How the game should look, and how the characters (other players, range dummies, and the player's own arms) are built.

![Both teams in several poses, the F4 hitbox overlay, and close-ups of an enemy and an ally](media/characters.jpg)

## The look

The target is a **Treyarch Black Ops game (Black Ops 2 and 3) built with the craft of the modern Modern Warfare games**: bold colour, a strong key light, clean readable shapes, and punchy effects, finished with photoreal materials and wear, grounded bounce light, detailed weapons, dense dressing, and effects that linger. Where the two pull apart, readability and colour win. The full target, what to leave out, and how to check a change are in the cod-design skill's [graphics reference](../.agents/skills/cod-design/references/graphics.md).

The game aims for **grounded stylized realism**: real materials (brick, concrete, painted steel, worn wood) on simple, readable shapes, under clean light. Surfaces use CC0 PBR texture sets, so they read as what they are up close. Shapes stay low-poly and hard-edged, so silhouettes and sightlines stay clear and the game stays cheap to run (see [performance.md](performance.md)).

Rules for every character, prop, and map:

- **Read the team at a glance.** The two teams differ in value first: allies wear light tan and olive, enemies wear dark charcoal. They differ in hue second: allies have a blue accent and enemies a red-orange accent, on the helmet band and the armbands. Value contrast survives colour blindness, fog, and distance; the accent confirms it up close.
- **Make the head obvious.** A helmet with a coloured band, dark goggles, and a face mask give the head a shape and colour that differ from the torso, so headshots are aimable at range.
- **Keep shapes simple.** Gear is a few dozen faceted triangles each: a helmet dome, a plate carrier with three magazine pouches and a radio, a belt with side pouches, knee pads, and armbands. Avoid noisy camouflage patterns that break up the silhouette.
- **Keep accents rare.** The team colour appears only on the helmet band and armbands, with a little self-light so it reads in shade. Everything else is muted.
- **Texture detail stays quiet.** A texture adds material, not noise: no high-contrast patterns, decals, or graffiti where players look for enemies. Characters must stay the most contrasting thing in a scene.

### World surfaces

![Slamcity and the Testing Area before (left) and after (right) the PBR world textures](media/world-textures.jpg)

Level geometry uses one shader, `godot/shaders/world.gdshader`. It projects a texture set in world space from the axis each face points along most, so boxes need no UVs, textures never stretch when a box is resized, and each pixel samples its textures once. Low-frequency noise varies brightness and roughness across large surfaces, so repeats don't show, and walls darken toward the ground with grime.

`tools/build_textures.py` builds the library from the tables in `tools/world_looks.py`:

- **Texture sets** (`godot/assets/textures/<set>/`): pinned CC0 sets from [ambientCG](https://ambientcg.com) at 1024², packed into an albedo, an OpenGL normal map, and an ORM map (R ambient occlusion, G roughness, B metallic). A set marked `grey` stores brightness only, so one set serves many paint colours.
- **Looks** (`godot/materials/world/<look>.tres`): a set plus a tint, the texture's size in metres, roughness and metallic scales, and how much grime. Maps reference looks by name. Slamcity has its own look table, `SLAMCITY_LOOKS` in `tools/world_looks.py` (see Slamcity below).

To add a surface, add a set or a look to the tool, run it, import, and run it again with `--fix-imports` (its docstring has the commands). Textures import VRAM compressed with mipmaps; normal maps use two-channel compression, and the shader rebuilds their blue channel.

Curved shapes switch projection axis where their normal turns past 45°, which shows as a seam on strongly patterned textures. Use calm textures on cylinders, or give modelled props their own UVs.

### Skyline windows

![Slamcity's skyline before (left) and after (right) the windows](media/skyline-windows.jpg)

The skyline buildings beyond the boundary are plain boxes, so the world shader draws their windows: a look with a `windows` entry in `tools/world_looks.py` uses the `world_windows.gdshader` variant and gets a grid of framed windows with sills on every wall above the ground floor. Behind each pane is a room that exists only in the shader (interior mapping): the view ray is intersected with a box behind the glass, and the wall, floor, or ceiling it hits is coloured, with a door or shelf on the back wall, blinds drawn part way in some windows, and a few rooms lit warmer than the rest. Every room differs by a hash of its cell, the glass reflects the sky through the engine's own specular light, and the edges are antialiased from screen-space derivatives so distant windows do not shimmer. It costs no geometry or textures, and parallax makes the rooms move correctly as the player walks past.

On the buildings players fight around, a fake window could read as a firing position. Slamcity's panes there keep their blinds drawn and their rooms dim (see Slamcity below); elsewhere, keep fake windows off those buildings.

### Character relief

Characters carry their detail in baked maps, not in the shader. `tools/build_character_assets.py` bakes each kit into one texture atlas: albedo, a tangent-space normal map (cloth folds, ripstop, MOLLE webbing, Cordura, stitching, and leather grain), and ORM (ambient occlusion, roughness, metallic). See [Building a character](#building-a-character).

### Props

![Covered cars, street lamps, crates, bins, barrels, jersey barriers, and roof plant across the three maps](media/map-props.jpg)

Props are CC0 models from [Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/models), built by `tools/build_props.py` in Blender: it downloads the 1K glTF, keeps the named parts, decimates to a triangle budget (1.5k to 6k; the textures carry the fine detail), puts the origin at the bottom centre, and writes `godot/assets/props/<name>/<name>.gltf` with its textures beside it. Godot generates LODs at import.

Props never change how a map plays. Each one that should block players or bullets stands on a box in a hidden CSG combiner (`Blockers`, `CoverBlockers`, or the map's existing combiners, now hidden), so collision stays exactly as simple as the blockout and impacts still report a surface. Crate stacks are scaled to fill their old blockout box; jersey barriers are scaled to fill the old low cover.

### Modelled game pieces

Everything a player looks at is a modelled mesh, built in Blender; Godot's primitive meshes and CSG stay for collision, effects, and debug shapes only. The Testing Area (a concrete training range: cast slabs, precast walls, interlocking-block cover, a lined culvert, a clad steel platform and ramp) and the Zombies arena (a walled compound at night: block walls between brick piers under barbed wire, boarded window bays with rubble-strewn alcoves behind, brick columns, broken slabs on the dirt) are `map_*` models, built the same way and baked into their lightmaps; each sits exactly on the map's old CSG, which stays as hidden collision. The game's own pieces (the sentry, the weapon racks, the Zombies window boards and door barricades, the power-ups, the Capture the Flag pole and plinth, and the hidden laser with its vault and pedestal) are procedural models in `tools/gear_models.py`, built with Slamcity's model API (bevelled boxes, hulls, subdivided cages, lofts, tubes, and 3D text). `blender -b --python tools/build_models.py` writes each to `godot/assets/models/<name>/<name>.gltf`, and its materials, folded from the same looks as Slamcity's, to `godot/materials/models/`. Those project their textures in the model's own space (the world shader's `object_space`), so a turning sentry head, a spinning power-up, or a board torn off a window keeps its texture as it moves. The laser draws through the viewmodel shader, which reads standard materials, so it exports plain glTF materials instead. `tools/test_models.py` fails in CI when a model is missing or older than its source, or when a prop, Zombies piece, or weapon scene builds a primitive mesh again.

### Set dressing

![The Testing Area before (left) and after (right) its set dressing: the armoury line, the control tower, the impact area, and the site office](media/testing-dressing.jpg)

The Testing Area is dressed as a working training range, so it reads as a place rather than a box: a steel-legged control tower in the north-east corner with a ladder, a landing, and a range flag on its roof; a site office against the south wall with lit windows; an ammunition container on the east wall, with a locked wall rack of four rifles on its side (`scripts/wall_rack.gd` stands the same third-person models players carry in it, LODs and all); target stands and hung steel plates in front of the east wall's impact area, with strike marks on the wall behind them (each plate is its own model on its chains and swings back when a round strikes it, then settles over a few seconds: `scripts/steel_plate.gd`, told about every impact by `CombatEffects`; besides the steel impact's own sparks, a hit on the face throws a bright splash of fragments out across it, `scenes/effects/steel_splash.tscn`); a sandbagged fighting position across the north-west corner and bags banked against the culvert's mouths; pallets, barrels, and a covered car by a sliding gate in the south wall; floodlight masts on the wall corners; a cable tray along the east wall and a cable protector across the floor; drains, wall scuppers, painted lane numbers and a hatched keep-clear box, brass along the armoury line, litter, and weeds in the slab joints and along the walls.

`tools/map_dressing.py` builds it as `map_testing_dressing`, with the same model API as the architecture. Three things keep it cheap and keep repeats from showing:

- **Merged by area, split by role.** `tools/build_models.py` writes a dressing model as several meshes, its parts sorted into quadrants by where they stand: each quadrant's large parts bake into the lightmap; its small parts (`detail`: litter, brass, weeds, strike marks, sign lettering) go into a `_detail` mesh that casts no shadow, takes light from the probes, and hides beyond 30 m; and what lies flat on the floor (slabs, joints, paint, stains, tracks) goes into a `_ground` mesh that casts no shadow, since the sun's shadow map would otherwise draw the whole floor for nothing. Repeated props (sandbags, pallets, target stands) are built in place inside these merged meshes, so a hundred of them cost a handful of draw calls.
- **Every slab and panel shaded on its own.** The floor is one quad per 4 m slab and each wall panel one quad, each with its own tint and roughness in its vertex colours, with grime banked toward the walls and cover and broad damp patches. Dark streaks run down from the copings and rust bleeds from the scuppers as thin strips laid over the panels in the same material. Finer grids were tried first: their distant slivers cost 0.3 to 0.6 ms. The floor's texture repeats every 5.3 m, not every 4 m slab, so no two slabs show the same patch, and each saw-cut joint is one connected strip whose width changes slab by slab and whose shade wanders along it (dirt-filled here, pale sealant there). Thousands of loose joint pieces broke the lightmap bake (every shade came out black), so each joint line stays one piece. The perimeter walls are precast panels slotted between cast posts 32 cm wide and 12 cm proud, so the sun draws a shadow down every joint; the posts collide (`WallPosts` in `scenes/main.tscn`), so rounds strike their faces, and the cable tray and wall signs hang clear of them.
- **Stains without decals.** Oil, scuffs, and tyre tracks are fans and strips in the floor's own material, darkest (and, for oil, glossiest) in the middle and fading to the slab's colour at their rims, so they blend in for no per-pixel cost; a Decal would turn on decal shading for the whole frame.

![The Zombies arena before (left) and after (right) its set dressing: the south wall's barricades, the burnt-out car by the west gate, a window bay, and the yard](media/zombies-dressing.jpg)

The Zombies arena (`map_zombies_dressing`) is a compound under siege at night: barricades of sandbags and nailed boards along the walls between the windows, a burnt-out car against the west wall and one in each side yard (Slamcity's car body with its paint burnt to soot and rust), rubble at the walls' feet, boards torn off the windows lying beside the way in, blood dragged in from every window, ammunition cans, packs, helmets, and brass dropped round the middle, wet streaks down the walls from the coping, emergency beacons over the gates, and floodlights clamped to the four big columns. Their light is baked into the lightmap (`WorkLights`, removed in the game by `scripts/baked_lights.gd`), aimed out at the walls the zombies come over, so the barricades and windows read at night for no frame time. The arena's floor is a `_ground` mesh that casts no shadow, and its architecture has a shadow-only twin, like the Testing Area's. The windows' climb-in points, the gates, and the six metres in front of them stay clear.

Anything a player could walk into has a box in the scene's hidden collision (in the Testing Area `MapMetal`, `MapWood`, `CoverBlockers`, and `MapDirt` for the sandbags; in the arena `Barricades` and the cars' blocks), so collision stays as simple as the blockout's.

### Slamcity

![Slamcity: Slam Avenue towards Slam Junction, the derailed car under the platforms, a platform, the fire escape off the north street, the diner, and the market](media/slamcity-junction.jpg)

Slamcity is a city block at dusk round Slam Junction, a station on an elevated railway. Its layout comes from studying real Call of Duty maps ([map-design.md](map-design.md)): city blocks you can enter with streets cut between them, round a central plaza. Every surface of it is generated, so the whole map is rebuilt from code:

- `tools/slamcity_layout.py` is the one description of the map: collision, visual parts (bevelled boxes, hulls, cylinders, tubes, 3D and neon letters, pictures from the sign atlas, and window panes), placed models, props, decals, baked lights, spawns, lanes, and zones. Walls are written once with their doors and windows (`Wall`), and Red's half is Blue's turned half round the centre, dressed as different places with its own names, colours, and interiors (`Side`): the tenement is a hotel, the diner a laundromat, the pawn shop the ticket hall.
- `tools/slamcity_models.py` models the cars, buses, trucks, the train, water towers, the shop interiors, and the street furniture from the same primitives. Solid stairs are one stepped extrusion (the `extrude` part: any flat polygon swept along a vector, its caps split into convex pieces), so their sides bake without seams. `tools/build_slamcity_city.py` runs in Blender, builds every part and model, merges them by chunk (a 3 x 2 grid, a finer grid for small things, and the skyline) and by material, and writes `godot/assets/maps/slamcity/city.gltf`. Each chunk also gets a shadow-only twin with all its opaque geometry in one surface.
- Looks come from `tools/world_looks.py`. Textured looks that share a texture set and scale share one material, their tint in the vertex colour; plain colours and lights fold into three palette materials. The plain colours (and the vehicle paint, more lightly) carry a shared smudge map, `world_wear` (`assets/textures/noise/wear.png`), that only ever makes them a little rougher and darker, so counters, appliances, and fittings are not flat colour. About 50 materials cover the map. `godot/tools/slamcity_import.gd` swaps them in at import.
- Windows players cannot use are panes with a room behind the glass (`shaders/window_pane.gdshader`, the same interior mapping as the skyline). On the buildings players fight around, their blinds are drawn and the rooms dim, so they never read as firing positions.
- `tools/build_slamcity_signs.py` draws every billboard, poster, shop sign, package, and route map into one atlas. Every brand and name on them is made up.
- `tools/build_slamcity.py` writes the scene and refuses a layout that fails `tools/slamcity_checks.py`: collision must match its twin turned half round (except the furniture of rooms each half dresses differently, and the derailed car across the centre); no spawn point may be in sight of the enemy's spawns, upper floor, platform, train, or roof terrace; no line longer than 50 m may run clear at eye height across the streets and the plaza, between the teams' high ground (lines that thread both rows of the stalled train's windows are allowed: keyholes, not lanes), and none longer than 35 m from a platform down its own team's streets; at least 15 spawn points must hide from the team's own high ground and 8 from an enemy pushed into the middle of the team's half. Every solid collider and stair blocks sight in these checks, the derailed car's turned boxes included. CI runs them (`tools/test_slamcity_layout.py`), with hashes that catch a scene, mesh, or lightmap left stale against the layout; `godot/tests/slamcity_test.gd` checks the spawn sightlines again with real physics rays and bakes a navigation mesh to prove bots can reach every zone and flag.

![Slamcity's plan as a minimap: Blue's depot on the west edge and Red's on the east, the north street, Slam Avenue, and the market as the three routes, the tenement and hotel, the diner and laundromat, the pawn shop and the ticket hall under the El, and Junction Plaza with the derailed car in the middle](media/slamcity-plan.jpg)

The layout is 84 x 68 m. Blue spawns in the bus depot on the west edge and Red in the depot on the east; three routes join them: Slam Avenue into Junction Plaza (mid range, under the platforms), the north street through the pawn shop into the far market (close quarters), and the market through the ticket hall into the far south street. Buildings you can enter link the routes: the tenement (with an upper floor over the avenue and the plaza, reached by its stair or by the fire escape from the north street), the bodega, the diner (a roof terrace over the market), the record store, and the subway hall. Each team has its own stair up to its platform; the stalled train between them is the way across. Spawn to spawn is a 79 m walk, and both teams reach the plaza in 5.6 s.

Hardpoint rotates through four zones: Junction, the Pawn Shop, the Platforms, and the Ticket Hall. Junction and the Platforms are their own twins; the Pawn Shop and the Ticket Hall are each other's, so their lean towards one team cancels over a rotation. Every move goes at least 16 m to a zone whose footprint does not touch the last one's (`check_hardpoint_hops`).

The map's set piece is the derailed car: a car of the stalled train that came off the El at the station and lies across the plaza under the platforms, sunk to its floor in the paving, its steel torn and scorched at the nose, and its nose still burning (`godot/scenes/effects/crash_fire.tscn`: flame tongues (`shaders/flame.gdshader`: additive, tapering, torn by upward-scrolling noise) and embers over a baked glow, under a smoke column of large noise-edged, sunlit puffs that bends away over the roofs, `smoke_plume.tscn` with `shaders/smoke.gdshader`). Players walk through it by its doors and its torn nose; wreckage across the aisle between each pair of facing doors keeps it cover to move through rather than a window across the plaza.

#### Decals and trim

Slamcity's road markings (the crossings, centre lines, edge lines, and parking bays) are thin painted strips in the city mesh, baked into the lightmap: as decals they cost about a third of a millisecond. Its other surfaces carry CC0 ambientCG decals (`tools/build_decals.py` packs them into `godot/assets/textures/decals/`): patched and broken asphalt, manholes, rain streaks under the roof edges, and puddles and oil stains the tool generates (their ORM map makes puddles nearly mirror-smooth, so they catch the sky). Following the rule that texture detail stays quiet, there is no graffiti where players look for enemies.

Visual trim has no collision, so it never changes movement or cover: window frames, sills, lintels, cornices, quoins, and downpipes on the buildings.

### Light

![Slamcity, the Testing Area, and Zombies before (left) and after (right) the lighting pass](media/lighting.jpg)

Each map is lit by a CC0 sky image (Zombies: a sky drawn in code) (an HDRI from [Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com), downloaded by `tools/build_skies.py` into `godot/assets/skies/`) and one directional light standing in for its sun or moon. The tool prints where the sun sits in each image; the map rotates its sky (`Environment.sky_rotation`) so the sun lines up with its light, and tilts the light to the same elevation. The sky also lights the shade (ambient light from the sky), so shadows pick up its colour instead of a flat grey.

| Map | Sky | Mood |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testing Area | `kloofendal_48d_partly_cloudy_puresky` | Clear midday with clouds, high sun, light haze |
| Slamcity | `kloppenheim_06_puresky` | Dusk: a low sun due south behind the storefronts (9°, above the sky's own 5° so the streets keep some light), long shadows, warm haze, and neon, lit windows, and street lamps baked into the lightmap |
| Zombies | Drawn in code (`shaders/night_sky.gdshader`) over a rendered skyline | Clear night: a moon in the south-west, stars, thin moonlit cloud, and a skyline of tree belts and a town with lit windows against town glow; blue moonlight, dim fog pooled in the side yards and window bays (High), and street lamps that flicker (`scripts/lamp_flicker.gd`) |

![The Testing Area's horizon, and the Zombies yard and sky, before (left) and after (right) the skies drawn in code](media/map-skies.jpg)

Zombies' sky is drawn in code (`shaders/night_sky.gdshader`): a dark gradient with town glow low on the horizon, stars, thin moonlit cloud, the moon, and the country round the arena against it. The moon stands on the bearing the scene's moonlight comes from, so shadows fall away from it, but it is drawn lower (`moon_elevation`, 24°) than the light (61°), where a player looking over the walls sees it; drawing it lower moves no light and costs nothing, while moving the light itself that low lengthened its shadow cascades over the horde (0.07 ms on Medium). The skyline is a strip rendered in Blender (`tools/build_horizon.py --night`): belts of broadleaves and conifers on rising ground, and in the town's direction (where the sky's glow is) apartment blocks and houses with a few lit windows, sodium street lamps along three streets, a factory with two chimneys, a water tower, a church spire, and a line of electricity pylons, with red lights on the tall things. The arena's 5 m walls hide everything below about 8° from the middle of the yard, so the skyline is made of tall shapes. It stores coverage and distance like the Testing Area's, and far shapes fade into the night haze. Where the skyline covers the sky the shader skips the sky behind it, and to pay for its two texture reads Medium reads the cloud's noise once instead of twice (High keeps both; `fine_clouds`). On Low it draws only the gradient and the moon, with no skyline (`scripts/map_render.gd` turns its `detailed` switch off). The sky never uses time, so its reflections are computed once and the moon shows in the puddles. On High, fog volumes pool in the side yards and the window bays where the zombies gather.

Beyond the Testing Area's walls lies open country, rendered in Blender into a horizon strip (`tools/build_horizon.py`) that the sky lays round the horizon (`shaders/day_sky.gdshader`): a valley floor rising to rolling hills and a far ridge, fields in a Voronoi pattern (pasture, dry grass, stubble, ploughed soil), hedgerows on the field boundaries, woods and copses of broadleaves and conifers, and a few farms. Every tree is a real mesh of a few hundred leaf clumps on visible branches, and the whole strip is lit by a sun at the map's elevation, so crowns are sunlit on one side and throw shadows on the fields; its middle column faces the map's sun whatever the sky's rotation. The strip (8192 × 448, a full turn from 3.5° below the horizon to 16° above) stores the land premultiplied by its coverage, and a second image stores each pixel's distance, so the shader fades the land into the map's haze as far as it really stands. It costs two texture reads on the sky's own pixels near the horizon, nothing measurable (`profile_frame.gd`); drawn as geometry, far less country cost 0.15 ms on Medium. No tree stands nearer than 110 m, so the missing parallax does not show inside the range. The sky's reflections and the lightmap still see the plain sky image. Rerun the tool after changing the map's sun elevation.

![Before (left) and after (right) the baked lightmaps: Slamcity's street, a corner store, the alley, and the Testing Area](media/lightmaps.jpg)

Each map also has a baked LightmapGI: three bounces of sunlight and sky light, so rooms, the walkway, and the shade under awnings get soft bounced light and darken where the sky can't reach. `tools/bake_lightmaps.sh` bakes it in two steps:

1. `godot/tools/bake_map_meshes.gd` turns any visible CSG left in a map into meshes with lightmap UVs (saved in `godot/assets/maps/<map>/`), places them beside the CSG, and hides the CSG, which keeps its collision and impact surface. Running it again replaces what it made. Every map is now modelled (the Testing Area and Zombies arena are `map_*` models from `tools/gear_models.py`, Slamcity is `city.gltf`), so it finds nothing to do; the CSG stays only as hidden collision. Models and props get lightmap UVs at import.
2. The editor plugin in `godot/addons/lightmap_baker/` opens each scene in the editor (switching to its tab if the editor stays on the project's main scene), presses its own Bake Lightmaps button, and saves. It needs a GPU (Xvfb works with one) and does nothing unless `BAKE_LIGHTMAPS` is set.

The baker can report success and still write an all-black lightmap when it loses the GPU partway (another process rendering at the same time), which darkens the whole map. A bake can also come out black on the map's own surfaces only (#240: the Testing Area's walls and floor, while its props kept their light). The sun is drawn live, so that shows only in shade, which turns black, and the atlas as a whole still looks lit. So the script then checks every lightmap it baked (`godot/tools/check_lightmap.gd`, using `godot/tools/lightmap_check.gd`): it fails on a black atlas, and it reads the texels each mesh's lightmap UVs cover and fails when more than 30% of the map's surface gets no light, or more than 40% of any mesh that holds 5% of the map's texels (healthy bakes: 6–16% and at most 25%). CI checks the committed lightmaps the same way (`godot/tests/lightmaps_test.gd`), and also checks that a copy of the Testing Area's lightmap with its walls and floor blacked out fails. Bake with the GPU otherwise idle.

Rebake a map after changing its geometry or light. Lights that exist only to be baked (Slamcity's ceiling lights in the flats and stores) sit under a node with `scripts/baked_lights.gd`, which removes them in the game: their light is already in the lightmap and its probes, which also light characters.

Zombies adds volumetric fog, which only the High preset draws.

### The graded picture

![The Testing Area, Slamcity, and Zombies before (left) and after (right) the grade, the bloom change, and the lens](media/graded-picture.jpg)

The final picture of a map is set in `godot/scripts/looks.gd`, not in its scene, so a map that a tool regenerates keeps it. For each listed map it holds:

- **Environment overrides**: the tone curve, exposure, haze, ambient occlusion, and changes to the shared bloom. The Testing Area uses the ACES filmic curve for hard daylight; Slamcity and Zombies keep AgX, which holds the hue of neon and lamps.
- **The sun** (`sun`): the map's sun can be set stronger against the sky light. The Testing Area's sun is 2.9 rather than its scene's 2.0, with less exposure, so sunlit ground nears white while shade goes dark; the baked bounce light stays as it was baked, and a little more sky light keeps soldiers and the first-person weapon readable in the shade.
- **Eye adaptation** (`exposure`, `scripts/eye_adaptation.gd`, every preset): the picture opens up in covered shade (an alley, a doorway, the culvert) by up to `brighter` stops and closes down by up to `darker` when the player looks into the sun. It is estimated from the world, not from the picture: eight rays from the camera into the sky say how much of it is open, and one toward the sun says whether the player stands in sunlight and whether the sun in view is hidden. So it costs no GPU time, and muzzle flashes and explosions can never pump it. A ray that starts inside a collider does not hit it, so where the camera can sit inside geometry (the Testing Area's culvert roof, below standing eye height) a thin collider inside the roof (`SkyCovers` in the scene) catches the rays and the spot reads as covered. The light it finds is compared with the level typical of the map's own views (`reference`: the Testing Area is open and sunlit, Slamcity's dusk streets are mostly shaded canyons), and the eye makes up 60% of the difference. It eases slowly (closing down twice as fast as it opens up) and only scales the exposure the map was graded at. Zombies only opens up.
- **Bloom** (`Looks.BLOOM`, the same for every listed map): only what is brighter than white blooms (`glow_bloom` is 0), and the glow is added like light scattered in a lens rather than screened over the picture. A faint wide level gives the sun, lamps, and muzzle flashes a soft halo. Zombies makes up for the lost veil with exposure, and blooms a little lower and stronger, so the horde reads and the lamps carry the night.
- **Lens dirt** (`scripts/lens_dirt.gd`): smudges and specks built in code from a fixed seed. The glow pass multiplies its bloom by them (`Environment.glow_map`), so the dirt shows only where something bright blooms through it.
- **A colour grade**: an S-curve on brightness around a pivot, a toe that deepens the darkest shadows without clipping them, a cool tint in the shadows and a warm one in the highlights that both fade out at black and white, and a saturation. A bright colour that would leave the range is pulled toward its own grey, so it keeps its hue. `Looks.build_lut` turns the grade into a 32³ look-up table when the map loads, and the renderer samples it inside the tonemap pass, so a grade costs nothing per frame. Tune a grade offline against a screenshot before you try it in the engine: the maths is a few lines. The renderer reads the table at texel centres with no half-texel correction, so texel *i* must hold the grade of input (*i* + 0.5) / 32 (`Looks.texel_input`); a table built as *i* / 31 darkens every shadow by more than 1% of full brightness.

| --- | --- |

Nuketown's scene and assets stay as imported; its look lives only here.

On top of that, for every map:

- **The lens** (`scripts/lens.gd`): a vignette and a little film grain, and on High a contrast-adaptive sharpening pass. The vignette deepens slightly while aiming, and on High the periphery blurs a little (never the middle, where the sights are). Rounds passing close (`suppress()`) close it in and drain some colour for a moment, and on High also blur the edges of the picture. After death the picture fades down. Low draws the lens only for those moments (suppression and death), with no standing vignette or grain.
- **The first-person key light** (`scripts/viewmodel_light.gd`): a shadowless key on the first-person weapon and arms only, the way a shooter lights its viewmodel. It takes the sun's colour and height, swings round over the player's shoulder, and grows as the player turns into the sun, so a backlit weapon still shows its shape instead of falling to flat grey. The weapon takes the sun's shadow like the world does, so in shade (one ray toward the sun, six times a second) the sun's key goes too, and the key becomes the sky instead: a light from above and ahead, in the sky's colour (a map lit by a flat ambient colour uses that colour), as strong as the map's sky light, so a black rifle and its gloves keep their shape and the sheen along the rail when the picture is exposed for the sunlit street beyond. Under a roof (a second ray, straight up) the sky's share drops to a quarter, and the room's own light, baked into the lightmap's probes, does the rest. The viewmodel shader adds it on the weapon's own pixels from two global shader parameters (`shaders/viewmodel_key.gdshaderinc`), and the first-person arms draw with a copy of the character shader compiled with the same key (`viewmodel::first_person_shader`), so soldiers in the world pay nothing for it; a real second light would cost every lit pixel of the world (0.16 to 0.28 ms on Medium).
- **The sun in the lens** (`shaders/lens_flare.gdshaderinc`, Medium and High): when a map's sun is in view, a glare around it, a few faint coloured ghosts on the line through the middle of the screen, and the lens dirt lit up near it. Five rays from the camera test whether the sun is hidden, so the flare fades as the sun slips behind an edge instead of blinking off. Each map sets its strength (`flare` in `MAPS`; the night has none).
- **Contact shadows** (`scripts/grounding.gd`): a soft dark patch on the ground under every soldier and zombie, so they stand on the ground in the shade and where the sun's shadow is soft or far. It follows the stance (a small oval standing, longer and darker crouched, laid out behind the feet in a slide, turned with the body), and in direct sun (a second ray, toward the sun) it fades to a faint core, since the sun's shadow already grounds the feet there. Each is a flat quad drawn with `shaders/contact_shadow.gdshader` and laid on the ground (tilted to a slope) by a short ray down from the feet a few times a second; it fades as the feet leave the ground and between 60 and 80 m. It reads no depth and is no Decal, so it costs only the pixels it covers.
- **The scope** (`shaders/scope.gdshader`): one pass that multiplies the picture: black outside the tube, a soft eye-relief shadow that slides to one side as sway and turning move the scope off the eye, a faint coloured fringe at the rim, glass that darkens toward its edge, and the cast of coated glass: it passes about nine tenths of the light, a little less blue than green, so the scoped picture reads as seen through glass, not as the screen with a ring round it.
- **Health**: the colour drains from the picture as health falls from 70% to 10% and comes back as it regenerates. Death is grey. This uses the tonemap pass's saturation, so it works on every preset. The damage vignette (`shaders/damage_vignette.gdshader`) multiplies the edges of the picture toward a dark blood red, so the middle of the screen stays clear and the sky never turns pink. Its edge is broken into smears that reach in from the rim, darker where they are thick; each hit darkens it toward black and pushes it further in for a moment, and below 35% health it beats with the heart (a double pulse, faster as health falls). At full health it is hidden, so it costs nothing.
- **Texture sharpness**: Medium and High read slightly sharper mipmaps.
- **World surfaces** (`shaders/world.gdshaderinc`): on High, a fine shared detail normal adds grain and roughness breakup within about 10 m, where the texture alone would blur. Only rough, non-metal surfaces take it at full strength, and each texture set has its own strength on top (`DETAIL` in `tools/world_detail.py`, written into each material as `detail`): dirt and broad concrete take the most, brick, planks, and canvas about half, paint, steel, and tiles a quarter or less, and tread plate none, so they keep their texture's own relief. Slamcity's materials keep the default until its tools write the same line. Big isotropic surfaces (asphalt, concrete, plaster) hide their repeat with a second sample shifted by a fraction of a repeat (`anti_tile`), blended in patches: shifted rather than turned, so its shading falls on the same side of its bumps as the first sample's. Low and Medium compile it out (see [performance.md](performance.md)). A map can be wet (`wetness` in `MAPS`): from Medium up, surfaces darken and turn glossy (walls less, and unevenly, with narrow vertical run-off streaks that stay soaked), and from half wet, puddles collect on the ground, with a darker soaked rim where the water thins out. The streaks and the puddles share one noise read (stretched down the wall, broad on the ground), so they cost a wet map nothing extra, and a dry map compiles all of it out. Zombies is just after rain.

What each of these costs is in [performance.md](performance.md).

### Weapons

![Every weapon at the hip, the M4 firing, its smoke, and the weapon racks](media/weapon-materials.jpg)

Every gun but the M870 is built from a detailed CC0 source model. From Stein Games' Free Classic Weapons Pack (rigged, about 10k to 17k triangles, 2K PBR textures): the AK-47, the M1911, the PDW-9 (an MP5), the BR-3 (an M14), and the M40 (a Remington 700, made military; see below). From 3dmodelscc0's Guns & Explosives Pack (parts as separate objects, 2K PBR textures): the M4A1. The M870 is modelled by the tool from real dimensions (`tools/weapon_m870.py`), since the pack's shotgun was a light sporting pump with its ports painted on. The LSW-6 is an RPK pattern derived from the AK-47: the tool stretches the barrel 175 mm ahead of the gas block, and models a folded bipod and a 75-round drum. The tool also models the suppressor (a 172 mm can with wrench flats, grip grooves, a vent ring, and a stepped cap) and the vertical foregrip (a rail clamp and a tapered grip with finger grooves) in metres, in the shared optics finish, and the fired cases that weapons eject (`CASES`: 5.56, 7.62x39 in lacquered steel, 7.62x51, 9 mm, .45, and a 12-gauge shell with a brass head), in `shared/casings.glb`. Each view scene names its case (`shell_model`).

The M870 (`tools/weapon_m870.py`, 6k triangles) is a service-pattern pump with an 18.5 inch barrel: a flat-topped 230 mm receiver that drops sharply into the wrist, with a cut 70 mm ejection port (the bolt face in it rides back with the pump), a loading port with its carrier and shell latch underneath, and the trigger group's pin heads; a guard with the trigger, a cross-bolt safety, and the action release lever; the barrel with its brass bead, the magazine tube with its cap and a barrel clamp; a smooth polymer forend with six grooves a side on the pump, with its action bars; and a polymer stock with a rubber pad. It is built in its own frame and moved into the weapon's view space, so the eye sits 13 mm over the receiver's top and the line to the bead is the sight line, as before; its markers come from the same tool. It is painted like the M4A1: phosphated steel, polymer, rubber, an anodised guard, a brass bead, and "M870 12 GA" engraved on the receiver's left.

The M40 is the pack's sporting 700 made into the military pattern (`militarize`): the free barrel thickened from 18 to 24 mm, the forend widened into a beavertail, a raised cheek piece on two posts, and the walnut (told apart by the source's colour map) refinished as olive glass fibre with a darker sprayed web, a rubber butt pad, and phosphated steel. Its riflescope has tall target turrets.

`tools/build_weapons.sh` rebuilds the set in one command. `tools/fetch_weapon_sources.py` downloads the source packs (pinned by size and SHA-256, cached in `~/.cache/shooter-weapons/sources`), and `tools/build_weapons.py` runs in Blender and, for each weapon in its `WEAPONS` table:

1. Imports the source in its rest pose. Every face goes to the part its bone moves (or, for a pack that splits parts into separate objects, the object it belongs to), so the magazine, the slide, the hammer, and the rounds in the magazine come out as their own nodes. For a magazine the source models empty, the tool adds the top rounds (`CARTRIDGE_PROFILES`).
2. Turns it to face -Z, scales it to the real weapon's length and sight radius, puts the rear sight's aiming point (the top of the notch) on the origin, and pitches it so the front post's tip lies on the -Z axis. Aimed, the post sits level in the notch exactly where the camera points, which is where shots land. A source whose sights sit off its centre line (the M4A1's, by 0.8 mm) moves sideways onto the line (`sight.x`), and `sight_edits` reshapes sights too small to read at game scale (see [Weapon sights](#weapon-sights)).
3. Repacks the source's maps into the game's layout at 2048 px: sRGB colour, an OpenGL normal map (DirectX-style sources are flipped), and ORM (occlusion, roughness, metallic, from the source's packed map or its separate ones). The source's own UVs stay. A source whose maps are flat instead has its finish painted from its geometry (`finish`, `tools/weapon_finish.py`; see below).
4. Exports `<key>.glb` in metres with one material for the weapon (`<key>.tres`), the shared cartridge material on the rounds, and the shared tritium glow on night sights, and `World`, the whole weapon merged into one node for other players' weapons and racks. A weapon with a magazine also exports `WorldBody` (`World` without the magazine) and `WorldMagazine` (the magazine alone, decimated the same way, with `Magazine`'s pivot): for the length of another player's reload they replace `World`, and the magazine follows the support hand's reload path (`carry::Carry::magazine_pose`): a rifle's or machine gun's rides the hand down to the vest pouch and back up into the well as it seats, and a pistol's drops out of the grip as a fresh one comes up from the belt. On an empty reload a rifle's or machine gun's old magazine drops free as the support hand reaches the well, and the hand brings a fresh one from the pouch (snapshots carry `PlayerView::reload_empty`); a tactical reload still stows it. A dropped magazine is a loose copy of `WorldMagazine` (`rust/game/src/character/magazines.rs`): it falls from where it sat, bounces once, and lies on its side for 5 s before shrinking away, at most six at once in a scene (the oldest is reused), one ray a frame while falling and none at rest, never drawn past 40 m and casting no shadow. The rest of the time a gun is still one draw call, except with the extended magazine: `World` carries the standard magazine, so a gun with the longer one always draws `WorldBody` and its own `WorldMagazine`, stretched 1.35 times along its length about its top as the first-person magazine is (two draw calls). The tool then writes the marker positions (`Muzzle`, `Ejection`, `OpticMount`, `GripRight`, `GripLeft`) into the view scene and a summary (`<key>.json`: triangles, nodes, markers).

The repacked textures keep weapon steel at a roughness of at least 0.32 and its bare-steel colour at most 0.6 (sRGB), so worn edges read as blued or parkerised steel rather than chrome, and every texture island's colour is grown 16 texels into the empty space round it (`pad_islands`), so mipmaps never pull the gaps' colour onto the edges. Each rifle's view scene also gets a `ChargingHandle` marker where the hand works the handle (the right-hand knob on the AK-47, LSW-6, and BR-3, the left-hand lever on the PDW-9, the T-handle's rear on the M4A1), which the reload clips reach for. The M870 aims over its receiver at a brass bead the tool adds on the barrel's end.

The M4A1's source maps are flat (one uniform grey, an almost empty normal map), so its finish is painted from the geometry (`tools/weapon_finish.py`). Each face gets a finish zone from rules on its part, position, and distance from the bore: hard-anodised aluminium (receivers, rail, charging handle, buffer tube), phosphated steel (barrel, pins, dust cover, trigger, selector), stippled polymer (grip and stock, from the pack's own metallic map), a grey GI magazine, an oily bolt carrier, and matte, barely worn sights so nothing glints in the sight picture. The gun gets fresh, even UVs (2.4 texels per millimetre; the source's were sheared on the magazine well), and Cycles bakes ambient occlusion, a normal map with machined edges rounded by a bevel shader plus the grip's stipple and fine scratches as bumps, an edge mask, and seamless 3D noise. The composition wears coatings through to bare metal on exposed edges, patchily, puts carbon in crevices and a little dust on top faces, lets scratches show only in reflections, and engraves the selector's SAFE, SEMI, and AUTO and a rollmark on the left of the lower receiver in Barlow Condensed (text is laid on the receiver wall and each texel near it is tested against the letters, at four points per texel). Letters are 5 mm, larger than a real rollmark's 3, so they stay legible at the texture's density. `WEAPON_FINISH_DEBUG=<dir>` keeps the baked masks for tuning.

At the hip each long gun is turned 5 degrees inward and rolled 4 degrees (`hip_rotation`, faded out as it comes up to aim), so its side, magazine, and firing hand show rather than only its top. An extended magazine stretches the magazine 35% along its length about its top, so it still seats in the well (`WeaponView::lengthen_magazine`).

Parts each rebuilt weapon exports (origins at the part's pivot, so a trigger or hammer turns about its pin):

In first person `WeaponView` moves these parts the way a modern Call of Duty viewmodel does (`animate_action` in `rust/game/src/viewmodel.rs`): the `BoltCarrier` runs back and home within each shot's cycle (an AK-pattern `ChargingHandle` rides the carrier instead, so it reciprocates), an AR's bolt locks open on the last round and slams home on the bolt release, the `DustCover` flies open on the first shot or charge and stays open, the `ChargingHandle` is drawn back with the hand on an empty reload's charge and the inspect's chamber check, the `Trigger` swings back about its pin on each shot and stays pulled through automatic fire, the pistol's `Hammer` falls on each shot and is cocked again by the slide, its `SlideStop` rises while the slide is locked back, and the `MagRelease` is pressed as the magazine comes out. The `Selector` and `Safety` stay put: the game has no fire-mode switch to move them. The `Bipod` and the M40's `BoltStop` stay put too. The M40's magazine change rolls it clockwise like a rifle's, the magazine toward the eye and the scope away. An AK-pattern `Magazine` (the AK-47's and the LSW-6's drum) rocks base forward about its front lip to come out, and goes in hooked on its lip and rocks back to lock, as the real magazine does; an AR's goes straight in. The AK-pattern rifles are held further out for the change (their deep receiver would otherwise fill the frame from an AR's distance), the LSW-6's drum seats with a heavier jolt, and an AR eases down out of the change's tilt and away from the eye for the bolt release, so the support arm stays low in the frame.

The M4A1, AK-47, LSW-6, and M1911 aim with their own modelled sights and the M870 along its receiver at its bead, so their view scenes have no `IronSights` node. The PDW-9 and BR-3 carry a reflex sight on their rails, with the sight line set at its dot. The M40 aims through a riflescope the tool models (`riflescope`: eyepiece, power ring, 30 mm tube, turrets, objective bell, rings, coated lenses) on its rail; its axis is the sight line. An optic sits on the `OpticMount` marker; when the mount is too low for it, the weapon hangs that much lower (`WeaponView::set_sight_drop`), so the optic's dot is on the sight line and the iron sights show in the bottom of its window. Weapon racks show the same third-person weapon other players carry, laid on its side (`WeaponPickup`), without a shadow (the table hides it, but every shadow cascade would still draw the gun). `World` is decimated to a fifth of the first-person triangles; every copy shares the weapon's imported material, and it drops to Godot's coarser LODs sooner than other meshes (`WORLD_LOD_BIAS`), since a gun is small beside the player holding it.

`godot/tests/weapon_models_test.gd` checks every rebuilt weapon's parts and markers, that its front sight's tip is within 0.6 mm of the sight line, and, looking from the aimed eye, that the iron sights make a clear picture (see [Weapon sights](#weapon-sights)). `godot/tests/capture_weapons.gd` captures each weapon at the hip, aimed, mid-reload, and inspected, in other players' hands, and on the racks, for reviewing, and `godot/tests/capture_turntable.gd` renders each model in a neutral studio from six angles, with wireframes, in first person and as `World`.

Each shot shows a flash seen down the barrel and a plume along it seen from the side (two quads crossed along the barrel), each a frame picked at random per shot from a 4 × 4 atlas of flashes rendered in Blender from glowing turbulent gas (`tools/build_muzzle_flash.py`, `assets/textures/effects/muzzle_flash_4x4.png`): a white-hot core with four to six ragged petals, and a plume that flares out of the muzzle and tapers to a point. Aimed, the flash draws from the atlas's smaller, tighter frames (the shotgun keeps its full blast), so it glows round the front sight without washing out the sight picture. Each class's flash is as big as its view scene's `Quad_flash`, and its plume 1.6 times as long: a pistol's or the PDW-9's short, a shotgun's long. The laser keeps its own flash, drawn in code. Every few shots a puff of smoke drifts up and fades in the world (`shaders/muzzle_flash.gdshader`, `shaders/muzzle_smoke.gdshader`, built in `WeaponView`). The smoke is a frame of the smoke flipbook lit by the scene; in first person each puff is placed in the world where the muzzle shows on screen and drawn with the world camera, so it stays where the shot was fired and walls hide it. After six or more rounds in quick succession, a thin wisp rises off the barrel for a couple of seconds once the firing stops. Other players' muzzle light shows only when the camera can see the muzzle, so a shooter behind a wall does not light the near side of it. Fired cases fly out of the ejection port and land in the world (see [Effects](#effects)).

### Effects

![Sparks and dust on a wall, bullet holes, motion blur on a fast turn, and muzzle smoke](media/effects.jpg)

- **Impacts** (`scenes/effects/impact.tscn`): chips of the surface (the explosion's modelled rubble chip, `assets/effects/chip.glb`, each stretched and shaded its own way from the surface's colour, so a pale concrete wall sheds pale grey flecks) that tumble, bounce on the floor below, and lie there before they shrink away (`shaders/debris.gdshader`; one ray finds the floor), and a dust puff in the surface's colour drawn from a smoke flipbook (`textures/effects/smoke_4x4.png`, lit by the scene, starting 10 cm off the surface so the wall does not cut it in half). Only metal sparks: six short, thin streaks that follow their velocity (`shaders/spark.gdshader`), and it does not chip. Concrete, wood, dirt, and glass give chips and dust, as in the reference. A round leaving the far side of cover (`TraceHit::exit`) throws a faster spray along its travel and a wider hole. Other players' rounds draw the same impacts where the client traces them (`ShotTracer.shot_traced`).
- **Bullet holes** look like what they are in: four variants for each surface class, with their own albedo, normal, and roughness maps (`tools/build_combat_fx.py`): chipped grey concrete with cracks, paint chipped back to bare steel, wood torn along the grain, cracked glass, and a dirt crater, each with a soot ring. They are 16 cm decals, picked and turned at random.
- **Marks that stay**: bullet holes and blood stay on the walls, as in Call of Duty, until the preset's budget is full; then the oldest is reused (Low 16 holes and 4 spatters, Medium 48 and 12, High 96 and 24). Everything comes from pools in `CombatEffects` (`rust/game/src/effects.rs`), never one node per shot, and characters and first-person models draw on a visual layer of their own (layer 2) that decals skip, so a hole never lands on someone walking past.
- **Blood** is dark and wet, never glowing: lit, alpha-blended droplets that stretch along their flight (`shaders/blood.gdshader`), most thrown out of the far side and some back toward the shooter, and a darker lumpy mist that puffs toward the shooter and hangs for half a second. A round that leaves someone with a wall within 2 m behind them spatters it (`textures/effects/blood_splat_*`, four generated spatters with a glossy wet core, sized by how far behind the wall is).
- **Tracers** (`shaders/tracer.gdshader`): a 7 m ribbon along the round's path, turned to face the camera, with a hot core, an orange glow, and a head brighter than its fading tail, bright enough to bloom. It never draws thinner than 2.5 pixels: widened past its true 1.8 cm it fills solid and dims to match, so a far tracer is a steady line. Every `tracer_every`-th round of each weapon is a tracer, for other players as for the local player, from a pool in `CombatEffects` (Low 8, Medium 16, High 24 in flight).
- **Fired cases** come out of the ejection port in first person and, after 0.09 s, are handed to the world where they appeared on screen (`effects::viewmodel_to_world`): they tumble, bounce off what they meet (one ray a frame while moving), clink positionally on hard bounces (quieter on soft ground), lie flat where they fall for 10 s, and shrink away. Other players' weapons throw theirs too, within 30 m. They are the modelled cases from `casings.glb` (brass, lacquered steel, the 12-gauge hull), from a pool of 12, 24, or 40 by preset (`CombatEffects::throw_case`).
- **Explosions** (`scenes/effects/explosion.tscn`, `CombatEffects.explosion(position, normal, size)`): built like a frag going off on dirt or concrete, not a fuel fireball. A short orange core that is gone in about half a second (Unity Labs' CC0 Explosion02HD flipbook from its burning frames on, at a bounded brightness, so the bloom gives it a halo without blowing out the picture; `shaders/explosion.gdshader`); a fan of jets of dark dirt (six on Medium and High, five on Low) and dust thrown up out of the ground, standing on their feet and tearing into clumps as they thin (a 16-frame flipbook rendered in Blender from lit scattering volumes, `tools/build_explosion_fx.py`, `assets/textures/effects/dirt_burst_4x4.png`, played by `shaders/dirt_burst.gdshader` with the scene's light, so it is brown in the sun, dark in shade, and never glows at night); a brown-grey dust cloud that rises and hangs for six seconds and a ring of dust rolled out along the ground (billows from the smoke grenade's flipbook); 36 chips of rubble (`assets/effects/chip.glb`, modelled in the same tool) thrown high that land and rest; a few short sparks; a seventh of a second of light; a scorch mark 3.4 m across with soot streaks, thrown dirt, and fragment pits (a decal, Low 4, Medium 8, High 12 kept); and a camera rattle within 22 m (`FpsPlayer.blast_shake`). About 22 quads and up to 36 chips, from a pool of three. Every frag grenade's blast (`frag_blast.gd`) plays it. `godot/tests/capture_explosion.gd` films it in the sun, in shade, and at night (`CAPTURE_EFFECT=smoke` or `flash` films the smoke cloud or the flashbang's pop).
- **Fire** (`scenes/effects/fire.tscn`, `CombatEffects.fire(position, size, seconds)`): upright flames playing Unity Labs' CC0 SmallFlame01 loop (`shaders/fire_flame.gdshader`, additive, soft where they meet the ground), dark smoke, embers, and a flickering light, burning for a set time and then dying down, from a pool of 2, 4, or 6 by preset. For incendiaries and burning wrecks.
- **Smoke grenades** (`scenes/effects/smoke_screen.tscn`, `CombatEffects.smoke(position, age)`): a bank of billows that pours out over two seconds, hangs, and thins away, each billow churning slowly through one of two Unity Labs CC0 Cloud loops (`shaders/smoke_screen.gdshader`, cross-fading between frames), lit by the scene per vertex as one round bank (each billow's corners take normals pointing out from the cloud's middle, so the side toward the sun is bright and the far side and underside fall into shade), each billow its own shade and thickness, the billows toward the rim eaten down to their denser parts so the edge breaks into wisps while the core stays opaque, and fading over its lowest 0.9 m so it has no hard line on the ground (on High it also fades against walls, with the gun smoke's soft edge). Inside it a grey fog covers the screen and the billows are not drawn. 14, 18, or 26 billows by preset, smaller where there are more; a pool of 2, 3, or 4 clouds. `age` starts a cloud part-way through, for a player who joins after it popped. The grenade's own scene (`scenes/equipment/smoke_cloud.tscn`) plays the hiss and stands for the cloud.
- **Flashbangs** (`scenes/effects/flashbang.tscn`, `CombatEffects.flash_pop(position)`): a white-hot, star-shaped burst for less than a tenth of a second (one of the Blender-rendered muzzle flash atlas's head-on flashes, picked at random), a spray of thin white sparks, a hard 0.15 s flash of light, and a small grey puff from the same Cloud flipbook, shaded as one round puff, that churns and drifts for a couple of seconds. A pool of two.
- **Gun smoke** (`shaders/muzzle_smoke.gdshader`, first and third person): on High each puff fades out over its last 35 cm in front of whatever is behind it, so smoke drifting into a wall or the ground thins out instead of cutting a hard line across it. It reads the depth buffer, which the firefight measurement found already copied while anything fires (+0.01 ms at best on High); Low and Medium compile it out (`GraphicsQuality.set_soft_smoke`). A heat haze over hot barrels was priced in the same firefight at +0.4 ms on High (it needs a copy of the screen) and left out: Slamcity's High worst view has no room for it.
- **Ambient dust**: a few dozen motes drift in the air around the player and catch the light.
- **Motion blur**: an option in the settings, off by default.

Depth of field while aiming was tried and left out: the weapon is drawn into a thin slice of depth (so it never clips into walls), so a near blur can only blur the whole weapon, iron sights included, which makes aiming worse.

### Palette

Every vertex of a character carries its palette role. Cloth and gear roles are *tinted*: the atlas holds only their shading detail, centred on mid grey, and `rust/game/src/character/palette.rs` gives each team a colour per role. Skin, eyes, lenses, and hardware (`metal`) keep the atlas colour. `shaders/character.gdshader` draws every role of a kit with one material per team.

| Role | Allies | Enemies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `uniform`, `pants` | tan (0.60, 0.53, 0.40) | charcoal (0.22, 0.23, 0.24) |
| `vest`, `helmet` | coyote (0.50, 0.44, 0.32) | near black (0.13, 0.14, 0.15) |
| `webbing` | ranger green (0.40, 0.40, 0.30) | near black (0.11, 0.12, 0.12) |
| `gloves`, `boots`, `mask` | brown and tan | black |
| `skin`, `eyes`, `visor`, `metal` | the atlas colour | same |
| `accent` | blue (0.16, 0.56, 1.0) | red-orange (1.0, 0.24, 0.10) |

Teams differ in value first and in accent hue second. A faint rim in each team's accent colour (strongest for the dark enemies) keeps a silhouette apart from a dark background at night and indoors.

### Character detail

![The zombie horde, two zombies up close, an ally and an enemy in boots, and an enemy's bare head under the helmet](media/characters-v4.jpg)

`shaders/character.gdshader` reads the kit's atlas and:

- colours each tinted role from the team palette, with faded patches that differ per character;
- collapses to a point, shadows included, the roles a team does not wear (a zombie's helmet, eye protection, plate carrier, and belt) and the part groups a character does not wear (`hidden_parts`: a balaclava, a neck gaiter, or a bare face, and the skin each covering replaces);
- multiplies the skin by the tone that goes with the soldier's face (`skin_tone`), varies it a little more per character, and for zombies drains the skin to a sallow pallor (values pressed together, so dark skin is lifted out of black and painted hair still reads) and darkens and dirties the clothes.

The per-character seed is an instance uniform (`variant`, `hidden_parts`, and `skin_tone`, set by `SoldierRig`), so every character of a team still shares one material.

**Faces.** A soldier wears one of five faces. The build makes the same MakeHuman human again with other macros (`heads` in the soldier kit: a dark-skinned man, an East Asian man, an older man, and a lean man of mixed descent), fitted the same way, so each has the base human's topology. It stores each one's difference from the base head as a blend shape on the near mesh, `Body` (`add_head_shapes` in `tools/blender_characters.py`): weighted by the head bone, aligned on the eyes, and never further out than the base head under the helmet or in front of the eyes where the glasses sit. The offsets ride through decimation and the face coverings as vertex attributes, so a balaclava or gaiter takes the face's shape too, and they are written as sparse glTF morph targets. `SoldierRig` picks a face per character (`palette::face`, from its seed, independent of the covering), blends 70 to 100% of the way to it, and sets the skin tone that goes with it (`HEAD_TONES`). The far mesh has no blend shapes: past 15 m a face's shape does not show. `capture_characters.gd`'s `faces` set shows each face bare, per team.

## Building a character

### Sources

- The skeleton, rest pose, and animations: Quaternius's [Universal Animation Library](https://opengameart.org/content/universal-animation-library) and [Universal Animation Library 2](https://opengameart.org/content/universal-animation-library-2), both CC0. The free Standard tiers include a mannequin on a humanoid rig and about 90 animations.
- The body, skin, eyes, and clothes: [MakeHuman](https://static.makehumancommunity.org/assets/assetpacks/index.html) CC0 asset packs, built with MPFB 2 (MakeHuman for Blender, GPL-3.0, used only as a build tool). The tool pins each download by hash. The MakeHuman file server refuses a bare browser user agent, so the tool sends `curl/8.0`.
- The kit (helmet with cover and mounts, glasses, balaclava and neck gaiter, plate carrier with pouches, magazines, radio, padded straps, and a hydration carrier on the back, a padded belt with a holster, hip and first-aid pouches, knee pads, knuckle guards, team bands): modelled in Blender Python by `tools/blender_gear.py`. The plates stand about 4 cm proud of the chest and back, and the hydration carrier 6.5 cm more, so the kit has a loaded soldier's bulk instead of sitting flat on the body.
- The build: a loaded soldier's, not MakeHuman's slim default. The macro has more muscle (0.85) and weight (0.62), and region targets (`targets` in the kit: MakeHuman modifiers by name, loaded before the rig is fitted) thicken the chest, back, and neck, the upper arms and forearms, and the thighs. It is girth only: `fit_to_skeleton` puts every joint back on the skeleton's, so the animations and hit-zone joints are unchanged. The arm and chest hit zones were widened to the new girth (upper arm 6.5 cm, forearm 5.5 cm, chest 21 cm radius), and the ragdoll capsules with them.
- The combat shirt and boots: modelled from the body and the fitted shoe by `tools/blender_wardrobe.py`, with high-detail sources baked onto them (below).

### Pipeline

1. Download the two animation libraries' Standard zips and extract them under `~/.cache/ual-src` (or pass `--ual1` and `--ual2`).
2. Run the asset builder: `uv run tools/build_character_assets.py`. It downloads and checks its other sources, installs MPFB 2 into a private Blender profile, and runs `tools/blender_characters.py` in Blender, which:
   - builds a MakeHuman human (`KITS` in the tool sets its proportions, skin, and clothes) and dresses it;
   - fits the body and clothes onto the soldier skeleton bone by bone, so the game's bones, rest pose, and proportions never change (`godot/tests/character_models_test.gd` checks every bone against `tests/data/soldier_skeleton.json`);
   - transfers skin weights from the mannequin, models the kit around the body (`tools/blender_gear.py`), and removes skin and cloth that the kit always covers. Hard gear follows one bone rigidly (the helmet, the belt's pouches, the bloused trouser cuffs); skinned gear (plates, straps, cummerbund, knee pads, holster) has its transferred weights smoothed over its own mesh (`smooth_weights`), so both faces of a thickened shell bend together instead of tearing apart. Each boot gets a modelled rubber sole (`boot_soles`): the boot's footprint widened 5 mm, with a sprung toe, a heel block, an arch cut, and a lugged tread in the baked relief;
   - packs every part into one atlas and bakes albedo, normal (procedural fabric, webbing, and grain), roughness, metallic, occlusion, and palette-role maps.

   The soldier's combat shirt (`tools/blender_wardrobe.py`) is two fabrics, as issued shirts are: a stretch-knit torso that sits under the plate carrier and ripstop sleeves, joined by raglan seams from the collar to the armpit. It starts as the body itself, cut cleanly at the hem (inside the trousers), the mock collar, and the cuffs, smoothed so no anatomy shows, and pushed out by how loose the cloth is there (snug on the torso and flat under the waistband, looser down the arms, firm at the cuffs), so every vertex keeps the body's shape and moves with the body's weights. Each sleeve has a pocket on the outer upper arm with a flap and a hook-and-loop patch field, a padded, quilted cordura elbow pad, and a cuff closed by a tab over the glove; the collar has a short zip. The team band goes round the arm between the pocket and the elbow pad. The game shirt is that shell subdivided once with the large forms as geometry, decimated after the carrier has covered what it covers (about 5,000 triangles). The high-detail shirt is the shell subdivided three times (about 160,000 faces) with compression folds inside the elbows, bunching above the cuffs, drape under the arms, tuck folds over the waistband, the felled raglan and underarm seams, every stitch line, the flap's lip, and the zip's teeth. It is baked onto the game shirt's atlas islands like the first-person gloves (`bake_pairs` in `tools/blender_hilo.py`). Fabric texture stays coarse (the ripstop's heavier threads, a soft knit grain): at the third-person atlas's 1.5 mm texels a finer weave only aliases into a grid.

   The combat boots (`combat_boots` in the same file) are modelled over MakeHuman's fitted `shoes03`, which gives the foot's shape and size: the shoe, cut below the ankle and capped, is united with a shaft that follows the leg (cast onto the rig's mannequin, since the body under the trousers is gone) 19 cm up the shin, and the two are voxel-remeshed into one closed shell and smoothed round the ankle so no step shows where they met. The trouser leg inside the boot is deleted and its last few centimetres taper in to the boot's top, where a bloused cuff (`blouses` in `tools/blender_gear.py`) goes round both. The high-detail boot (about 210,000 faces each) has a leather foot with a stitched toe cap and heel counter over a rubber rand, a nylon shaft, a gusseted tongue with eyelets every 19 mm and two laces crossing between them up a line measured from the boot's own front, a padded collar, a pull loop, and flex creases at the ball of the foot and the ankle. The game boot is about 1,200 triangles, weighted to the foot, toe, and shin with its weights smoothed, and sits on the lugged sole.

   The tool then writes `soldier/soldier.glb` (one mesh, `Body`, and its far copy, `BodyFar`, with one material `character_soldier`, on the original skeleton) and its atlas, `soldier/soldier_{albedo,normal,orm}.png`; `arms/arms.glb` with its own atlas, the first-person arms (the `arms` kit: modelled gloves and cloth-simulated sleeves, baked from high-detail sources, and a field watch on the support wrist, trimmed to the arms so the whole atlas goes to what the player sees; see below); and `zombie/zombie.glb` with its own atlas: the same body and clothes with bare hands and no kit, so nothing under a helmet or plates is cut away. `scenes/characters/zombie_body.tscn` wears it, and `scenes/zombies/zombie.tscn` instances that. It also writes `animations/ual1.glb` and `animations/ual2.glb`: the animations the game uses, on each library's own skeleton. `--kits soldier` rebuilds one kit, and `--reuse` reassembles from the last bake without Blender.
3. Import in Godot (open the editor, or run `godot --headless --path godot --import`). The `.import` files retarget the files onto Godot's `SkeletonProfileHumanoid` with `bone_map_ual1.tres` and `bone_map_ual2.tres`. Bones get the profile's names (`Hips`, `RightHand`, ...) under a skeleton named `%GeneralSkeleton`, and bone axes are normalised. Animations from either library then play on the soldier. Clips whose names end in `_Loop` lose the suffix and loop.

Each vertex stores its palette role and its part group in UV2. To add an animation, add its name to `UAL1_ANIMATIONS` or `UAL2_ANIMATIONS` in the tool and run it again (`--animations-only` rebuilds just the animation sets, without Blender). The lists hold only clips the game plays or authors from, since every shipped clip stays in memory; take one out when nothing uses it any more.

To review the characters, capture both teams at several distances and in several lights, mid-animation poses, turntables, wireframes, the first-person arms, and the horde: `CAPTURE_DIR=/path/to/out godot --path godot --resolution 1600x900 --script res://tests/capture_characters.gd` (needs a GPU and a display, or Xvfb).

### Runtime

The scenes that use the soldier:

- `scenes/characters/soldier.tscn`: `SoldierRig`, the third-person character. Range dummies (`scenes/props/target_dummy.tscn`) and other players (`scenes/remote_player.tscn`) instance it.
- `scenes/characters/fp_arms.tscn`: `FpArms`, the player's own arms. It is a child of the viewmodel in `scenes/player.tscn`.

`SoldierRig` (`rust/game/src/character/rig.rs`) takes one frame of state: velocity, floor contact, stance, sprint, view angles, aim-down-sights amount, weapon, reload progress, and whether the player is alive. Three parts turn that frame into a pose:

1. **An animation tree, built in code.** Speed-driven blend spaces for standing (idle, walk, jog, sprint) and crouching, with playback scaled to the real speed so feet don't slide, each in eight directions of travel (below). Further blends add the slide and the jump. An upper-body layer holds the arms in a firing pose. Deaths are ragdolls (below). The rig advances the tree itself, so a pose depends only on the time it is given.
2. **`SoldierPoser`, a skeleton modifier** (`poser.rs`) that runs after the animation. Standing still, it keeps the hips on the planted feet as the aim turns and turns the spine back toward the aim (strafes have their own cycles, below). It bends the spine, neck, and head with the aim pitch, applies hit reactions (below), and places the weapon relative to the eyes (hip, aim, sprint, reload, kick, switch). Two-bone IK then puts both hands on the weapon, and the fingers close in a shape for what each hand holds (`Hold` in `poser.rs`, chosen per weapon class, never per mesh): the firing hand wraps the pistol grip with the index on the trigger (curled further on a pistol, whose trigger sits closer to the grip), and lays it straight along the frame while sprinting or reloading; the support hand makes a loose C under a rifle's handguard, wraps a shotgun's pump, or cups the firing hand on a pistol. Each shape also sets how far each finger fans at its knuckle and how far the thumb turns in front of the palm (opposition), so the lower fingers spread down a pistol grip, the support fingers fan along a handguard with the thumb up its near side, and the fingers close up together round a pump, hooked round its far side so the fingertips stay below the receiver's top and off the bead when aimed. In first person, on a handguard or pump, the support palm rests on the weapon's surface: `grips_of` (`rig.rs`) cuts the weapon's shown parts at the support marker once per weapon, and the IK puts the palm on that cross-section instead of a fixed distance from the marker, so the hand fits whatever handguard a weapon has. The snap follows the marker along the barrel (a pump stroke) and lets go as a reload clip takes the marker away. In third person a soldier holding a long gun stands bladed: the upper chest turns 15° about the spine (`carry::BLADE`, about the aim-pitched up of `carry::blade_axis`), support shoulder forward, and the neck turns back as much so the head still faces the aim; it squares up in a sprint, while the support hand throws, and with a pistol. That brings the support shoulder about 5 cm forward and takes the firing shoulder as far back, so the stock comes out of the chest onto the shoulder and the support arm reaches up the handguard: the hand sits 2 cm behind the handguard marker (it sat 8 cm behind before, near the magazine well, because the square shoulders could not reach further), and the weapon sits 3 cm further forward of the eyes than it did (`carry::HIP_OFFSET`, `ADS_OFFSET`), so less of the stock is buried in the chest and neck. The hit zones blade the same way (`hitbox.rs`), so the arm zones stay on the drawn arms. Its palm rests on the handguard (or pump) there too, but on a section stored in the weapon file (`section` in its `grips`), because the server poses the arm hit zones from the same numbers (`carry::Grips::support_contact`, shared by the poser and `hitbox.rs`). `godot/tests/bake_support_sections.gd` cuts each two-handed weapon's third-person model (`World`) where that hand holds and writes the section; `support_sections_test.gd` fails if a stored one drifts more than 2 mm from the model, so re-run the bake after changing a third-person model or a `GripLeft` marker. On a vertical foregrip both the drawn hand and the arm zones move down onto the grip (`carry::FOREGRIP_SHIFT`).
3. **The weapon.** Each gun has one scene for both views (`scenes/weapons/*_view.tscn`). The `GripRight` and `GripLeft` markers and `grip_rake` say where the hands go. A view set to `world_model` skips the viewmodel shader and the sounds, and its muzzle flash faces the camera.

**Zombies.** A zombie's hit zones are posed hunched as the shamble draws it (`hitbox::Activity::hunch`): walking, the hips raised 4 cm and set back 10 cm, the spine bent forward 40° over them and the neck 6° more, the head turned back up to look ahead, and the arms hanging at the sides; standing still, a share of that hunch (0.65) on bent knees (a 0.36 crouch). The head zone sits within about 8 cm of the drawn head either way, where a soldier's pose put it 30 cm off. A heavy hit (force 8, about 75 damage, so shotguns and sniper rifles) staggers a zombie: it stops for 0.5 s and rocks back with `Hit_Knockback`. Zombies flinch at the part hit like soldiers, and die into ragdolls (at most four moving at once) that lie for 10 s.

**Weapon handling by class** (`hitbox::carry`, shared with the arm hit zones). Each weapon is carried as one of five classes: rifles and SMGs, light machine guns (held lower and canted, taking the weight), bolt-action rifles, pistols (held out at arm's length in both hands, carried muzzle down at the side in a sprint), and pump guns. A reload moves the support hand along its class's path in weapon space, timed to the share of the reload at which the fresh magazine seats (`ReloadTiming`): a rifle's hand goes to the magazine, pulls it down to a vest pouch, comes back up and seats the fresh one, then returns to the handguard; a pistol's drops to the belt as the empty magazine falls and comes back up to the grip's base; a pump gun's goes from a side pouch to the loading port and back for each of four shells. Mid-reload each class tilts its own way (rifles roll the well toward the support hand, pistols tip up, pump guns roll the port over). The hit zones pose the arms from the same path. A switch lowers the old weapon for 0.22 s, then the new one comes up over 0.3 s. Each shot kicks as hard as the weapon's first recoil kick relative to an M4 (0.6× to 2.2×) and pushes the shoulder and spine back 1.2° at a rifle's kick. `SoldierFrame::action` (plant, defuse, throw) kneels the body with the weapon lowered and a hand to the ground, or swings the support arm over to throw; nothing sets it until snapshots say who is planting or throwing.

**Transitions** (`rig.rs`). A slide drops in with `Slide_Start`, played from where the hips start down at twice its speed, so the body is down (about 0.13 s) as soon as the hit zones take the slide pose, and gets up with the rise of `Slide_Exit` over 0.25 s. A jump that leaves the ground pushes off with `Jump_Start` for 0.25 s on its way into the air loop; stepping off an edge goes into the loop only after 0.12 s in the air. A landing after more than 0.2 s in the air bends the knees with `Jump_Land`, fully after 0.6 s, for 0.55 s, held to a fifth of the clip's 46 cm squat (about 9 cm), because the hit zones do not crouch on landing. The torso leans into a start and back out of a stop, 6° at a walk's acceleration on a soft spring.

**Feet on the ground** (`SoldierPoser::plant_feet`). A soldier standing within 15 m of the camera casts one ray down under each foot. If the ground there is higher or lower than the ground the body stands on, the hips drop (at most 9 cm, to stay near the hit zones, which stand on flat ground) and two-bone IK bends each leg so its foot reaches the ground, keeping the foot's angle. Where the straightened leg still falls short (a foot over the next stair down), the foot points its toe down until the ball of the foot reaches the ground, up to 49° below level (`MAX_TOE_REACH`), as a person reaches for a lower stair; the hips stay within their 9 cm, and the leg zones' bones move at most the leg's last centimetre of straightening. On an 18 cm step the lower foot now stands on the ground; it hung about 4 cm over it. Moving bodies' feet follow their stride.

**Directional gait** (`tools/build_gait_animations.py`, `gait::DIRECTIONS`). Neither CC0 library has strafes or backpedals, so the walk, the jog, and the crouched walk are authored in seven more directions (45° apart) from their forward cycles, and the sprint in its two forward diagonals, into `animations/gait.glb`. Each ankle's path is turned about its mean position to the new direction, so the planted foot slides opposite the travel; the backward half starts from the forward cycle played backward (timed so each foot is planted over the same stride phases), so a backpedal reaches back toe first. The hips turn toward a strafe (20° walking, 28° jogging) and the spine turns back so the chest stays on the aim; the line between the feet turns the same way all round, furthest at the back diagonals (81°), so the trailing foot crosses in front and the travel never runs along the line between the feet, in any direction or blend of two. Two-bone IK puts each ankle on its path (the knee bends about its own hinge, then the leg swings to it); the hips sit up to 3 cm lower where a foot would be out of reach. Sideways the steps are shorter and quicker (cadence 1 + 0.2·|sin|), and the stride clock speeds up to match. Between two authored directions the tree blends their loops, and so their foot paths, which are 45° apart and shorter together than either (by about 8% halfway); the stride clock runs faster by as much (`gait::blend_cadence`), so a planted foot keeps pace with the ground instead of sliding along with the body (a walk halfway between forward and a diagonal went from sliding 7–11% of its travel to 0–6%, against about 1% in an authored direction). Behind the strafes, a blend of the strafe (from the forward cycle) and a back diagonal (from the reversed one) still plants less cleanly and slides about a fifth of its stance. The tree blends the eight directions by the travel's direction (eased round at 12 a second), each blending its walk and jog by speed; forward and its diagonals also have the sprint, so a strafe or backpedal stops its blend at the jog and plays it faster. A sprint drifting sideways (at most about 27° with the sprint's strafe share) blends the forward sprint toward its diagonal, whose hips open 21° and whose feet run along the drift; its direction is held within the diagonals. Before this, strafes ran the forward loop with the hips turned up to 75° and backpedals played it in reverse, and a drifting sprint ran the forward loop with the hips turned the whole way toward the drift. Reference: Call of Duty's third-person locomotion has eight-way walk, run, and crouch-walk cycles with crossover strafes and a backpedal that reaches back; Modern Warfare (2019) blends them by the travel's direction.

**Turning on the spot** (`gait::Turning`). Standing still, the hips and legs stay on the planted feet while the aim turns, and the spine, chest, and upper chest take the difference in the strafe's shares, so the chest faces the aim. Once the aim is more than 60° from the feet, they step round at 360° a second until they are within 5°; the aim is never more than 90° from them (a faster turn drags them). While the feet swing, the walk loop blends in up to 70%, a stride per half turn, so the legs step instead of sliding. The server's body clock runs the same rule and sends the aim's offset from the feet and the step (a byte each), and the hit zones turn the legs about the feet (not the hips, which sit off the line above them) and blend the measured walk poses by the step, so the zones follow the drawn body through a turn. Offline characters run the rule themselves.

**Hit reactions** (`SoldierRig::hit_at`). A hit that does not kill reacts at the part it landed on (`protocol::HitPart`, sent with every hit, or the hit shape our own shot struck): a head hit snaps the neck and head along the shot (about 18° for a rifle round), a body hit tips the torso along the shot (9°) and twists it away from the side hit (8°), an arm hit loosens that hand's grip on the weapon to 75% for 0.18 s, and a leg hit buckles the knees (the stance blend dips 0.3 toward a crouch and springs back). Each is a damped spring, scaled by the square root of the force level (up to twice a rifle's). The hit zones do not follow these flinches; they last a fraction of a second. When our own shot hits another player, their body flinches at once, and the snapshot that confirms the hit does not flinch it again (it only updates the push a death would take).

**Wound marks** (`wounds.rs`). Where a shot lands (the hit point every hit carries), the skin or cloth darkens to blood for 5 s, then fades over 3 s: a ragged mark about 8 cm across, darkest and wettest in the middle. A character shows its four latest marks. Each rides the bone nearest the hit, so it moves with the body, and goes to the shader as a point in the mesh's space and a strength (`wound_0` to `wound_3`, instance uniforms), where four distance tests per vertex draw it. The dead keep their marks; a respawn clears them. The first-person arms never get any.

**Deaths** (`ragdoll.rs`, `corpses.rs`). When the frame says a character is dead, the poser captures every bone's local pose as drawn that frame, and the next frame the animation tree and poser stop and twelve capsules take the body: hips, spine, chest, head (from the neck), upper and lower arms, and upper and lower legs, sized from the hit zones' radii, about 70 kg in all, joined by cone-twist joints (spine and chest 30° swing, 20° twist; neck 40°/45°; shoulders 80°/45°; hips 60°/20°) and one-way hinges at the elbows and knees (up to 140°). Bones between and past the parts (the upper chest, shoulders, hands, feet, fingers) keep their pose relative to their parent. The capsules sit on the "ragdoll" layer (8), which collides with the world only, so bodies never block players, bullets, or each other. Every part starts at the character's velocity (60% of its run along the ground) and the part the killing shot hit takes a push along the shot: 24 N·s plus 4 N·s per force level, a level being about a tenth of the damage dealt (a rifle round about 36 N·s, a close shotgun blast 84 N·s), a little less on the head. A shot body then buckles, as Call of Duty's deaths do, instead of toppling back stiff as a plank (which bowed the body back 30 to 65° in its first 0.3 s): for about half a second short-lived muscles (`Buckle`, pulled each physics tick by the ragdoll's holder node) bend its knees, hips and spine toward a plan chosen from the part hit and the side the round came from, then let go over 0.3 s. Shot from the front, the body hunches over the wound (55° of forward bend), its struck shoulder turns back and it goes down on that side; from behind it folds onto its knees and pitches forward; from the side it bends with the push and drops on the near knee; a leg hit gives that leg (105° against 50°) and the body goes down over it; a headshot folds the legs under it where it stands. A close shotgun blast or sniper round folds half as much and the push carries the body. The legs are free from the start, the spine and chest are braced for 0.08 s while the round's push spreads through the body and then open to their full range, and the arms keep 12% of their range for 0.18 s and loosen over about half a second. The muscles only turn parts against each other, so the body's centre of mass still goes with the push and gravity; `SoldierRig::fall_direction` reads it. A body that has stopped falling loses its speed along the ground as kit and cloth would drag it, down to 0.2 m/s; slower than that it is left to friction, because setting a body's velocity wakes it, and a drag written every frame never let a body sleep. Each frame the capsules' transforms are written into the skeleton's own bone poses. A body comes to rest once its limbs have settled about their rest and every capsule has been asleep or slower than 0.08 m/s (and 0.6 rad/s about itself) for a quarter second, which also catches a capsule rocking against a joint's limit or spinning slowly on the ground that physics sleep alone never stops. Once a body is at rest, after 5 s, or when a seventh ragdoll starts (a fifth in Zombies), the oldest freezes: its capsules are freed and the skeleton keeps the pose, so a corpse costs only its drawing. The held gun drops as a rigid body.

A remote player's body and a zombie's go to the scene's `CorpseYard` when they die, so a respawn gets a fresh body while the old one lies where it fell: soldiers for 30 s, zombies for 10 s, at most ten at once, then the oldest sinks 0.6 m into the ground over 1.5 s and is freed. Dropped guns lie for 15 s, at most eight. Range dummies fall and get up in place. The soft contact patch under the feet turns off while a body lies. Characters no longer flash red when hit.

![First-person arms: M4 at the hip, aimed, sprinting, and reloading, the pistol aimed, and the M870 pump](media/first-person-arms.jpg)

The first-person gloves and sleeves are built by `tools/blender_hilo.py`, because the player looks at them every match. Each glove starts from the MakeHuman hand, so the fingers keep MPFB's detailed finger weights, smoothed so no nails or creases show through. The high-detail glove is that hand subdivided four times (about 0.6 mm edges, 430,000 faces) and shaped from the skeleton's hand and finger frames:

- a moulded knuckle guard with a lobe over each knuckle, flex grooves, and vents, skinned to the hand bone alone so it does not stretch;
- a padded back panel with flex lines;
- pads over each finger's first two segments, with flex ridges over the middle joint;
- fourchette seams down every finger;
- a synthetic-leather palm and fingertip caps, with foam pads at the heel of the hand and under the knuckles;
- a neoprene gauntlet with a hook-and-loop wrist strap and a rolled hem;
- stitching along every seam.

The game glove is the same hand subdivided once with only the large forms (the guard, the pads, the strap), then decimated. Each sleeve is a tube cut wider than the arm, pinned at the shoulder and along a firm cuff band, and run through Blender's cloth solver against the arm while the fabric relaxes longer than the space it has. It buckles into compression folds above the cuff and slack folds along the forearm. The game sleeve is that cloth, decimated. The high-detail sleeve is the cloth subdivided, with a felled seam down the underside, a double-stitched hem, and a cuff tab. Albedo, normals, roughness, and occlusion are baked from each high-detail mesh onto its game mesh's atlas islands. Panel colours (leather, fabric, rubber, strap, thread) differ only in value, so the team's glove colour still tints them all. The kit is about 25,000 triangles. That is a soldier's budget, spent once, on the one model that is always on screen.

`FpArms` (`arms.rs`) uses the same poser and grip markers on its own model, `arms/arms.glb`, which holds only the sleeves and gloves. It draws them with the viewmodel shader (its own FOV, so it never clips into walls), and anchors the shoulders behind and below the camera. The arms are drawn 1.12 times life size so the shoulders can sit behind the camera, but the hands are scaled back to life size (`SoldierPoser::hand_scale`), so they are the size the grip offsets and the weapon expect. The viewmodel rig moves the weapon, and the hands follow it. During a reload the support hand leaves the weapon to fetch a magazine. While the support hand throws a grenade (the weapon is down out of the frame), the arms come back to life size, scaled about the support shoulder, and the body moves 8 cm up, 4 cm out, and 14 cm forward, as a thrower's torso turns the throwing shoulder forward (`throw_scale`, `throw_shift`). From the draw-back through the release the support elbow bends toward a point out beside the head instead of the weapon hold's (`throw_pole`, keyed by `grenade_hold::elbow_out`), so the forearm comes over from the left edge of the view at arm's length, as in an overhand throw, instead of rising through the middle of the view close to the eye. While the grenade comes up and cooks, and in the follow-through, the elbow stays down under it. `fp_throw_arm_test` checks the elbow is outside the view and the arms are life size as the arm comes over.

**Hand poses per weapon.** Call of Duty, like every shooter whose source or decompilation we read (Halo's first-person animations, Doom 3's view models, Team Fortress 2's arms, the grab-point poses of Godot's XR tools), authors the hands on each weapon by hand: no game we found solves fingers at runtime. We have no animator, so each weapon file carries a fitted pose (`hands` in `godot/data/weapons/*.ron`): every finger's curl per joint and spread at the knuckle, the firing index laid along the frame (other players' soldiers lift it off the trigger while sprinting or reloading; the first-person hand keeps it on), the support hand on a vertical foregrip for weapons that take one, and how far each hand sits off its grip marker. `godot/tests/bake_hand_poses.gd` fits them to the model (`rust/game/src/character/grasp.rs`) the way an animator would pose them:

1. The weapon's surface around each grip becomes a small distance field (4 mm cells, measured to 16 mm), cut from the meshes the first-person weapon shows. It is negative inside a closed part (every edge shared by two triangles), so a finger buried deep in a receiver reads as inside it, not as far from its surface; which side of an edge or a corner a point is on comes from angle-weighted pseudo-normals (Bærentzen and Aanæs), so the faces that meet there agree. An open part (a sheet) counts by distance only.
2. The hand slides onto the surface (at most 12 mm): the least move that takes the palm (tested as a plate) and every knuckle out of the weapon.
3. Every finger closes from nearly straight along its grip's shape (the `HandShape`s in `poser.rs`), all joints together, each joint stopping where the finger beyond it meets the surface. The gloved finger is tested as a chain of capsules measured from `arms.glb` (12.5, 11.5, and 10 mm radius from knuckle to tip, toward the pad), allowed to press 10% of its radius into the surface. A finger that meets nothing keeps the shape; thumbs close further, so they come to rest on the weapon's side.
4. Anything still inside is nudged out: up to 12° of curl and 6° of spread from where the finger closed (twice that for the trigger finger, which threads the guard), wider if that is not enough.

The game only reads the result. A hand's fitted pose and seat fade as it leaves the grip they were fitted to (the support hand fetching a magazine, the firing hand working a bolt) but not through a pump's stroke. Other players' soldiers pose the same firing hand and foregrip fist; on a bare handguard their support hand holds further back than the first-person one, where the fit does not apply, so it keeps the hold's shape. `godot/tests/hand_poses_test.gd` holds every weapon in its stored poses and fails if a sampled finger goes more than 1 mm into it or a palm more than 5.5 mm (a flat plate cannot wrap a narrow foregrip as a fist does; those read about 5 mm in), if a finger goes unmeasured, or if a weapon has no fitted poses. It does not compare the poses with a fresh fit, so a changed model passes as long as the stored poses still clear it. Re-run the bake after changing a model, a grip marker, or the gloves, then rebuild (the weapon files are compiled into the game). A few fingers the fit cannot clear are listed in the bake's `KNOWN_DEPTHS` with how far they may go in: the pistol's support index (5.5 mm) and one LSW-6 support finger (1.6 mm), not yet diagnosed. Fixing the model or the marker should remove each entry. The test also fails if the firing hand's middle, ring, or little finger closes less than 120° (summed over its joints) round the grip: a fit that stops early leaves the hand open beside the grip. Every weapon passes it. The cause was the grip marker and rake, not the fit: with no rake (the pistol until #215) the hand stood square to a raked grip and the fingers ran into the trigger guard and the front strap; on the M4A1 the marker sat over the magazine well, so other players held the receiver ahead of the pistol grip. Each `GripRight` now sits on its model's grip (measured by slicing the mesh across the grip), and each rake follows the grip's front strap: M4A1 22° (the marker 2.5 cm lower and 3.5 cm back), BR-3 32°, PDW-9 22°, M40 38°, LSW-6 22°. The markers live in three places that must agree: `tools/build_weapons.py` (which patches them into the scene), the view scene (`GripRight`, `grip_rake`), and the weapon file's `grips` (which the server's arm hit zones read; `hitbox_fit_test.gd` fails if they differ).

Values set by hand after the fit go in the weapon file's `hand_tuning`, outside the bake's `hands` block: the game applies them over the fitted pose (`HandTune::apply`), and the bake checks the poses with them applied (`FpArms.with_tuning`) but writes only `hands`, so a re-bake keeps them. They are the pistol's firing thumb, laid over the backstrap instead of standing up beside the slide, and the AK-47's support hand, seated 17 mm lower with its index bent at the tip instead of the knuckle, so the thumb and index stay below the red dot's window (fitted on the handguard's top edge, the thumb rose into the bottom of the window); and the LSW-6's support index, laid straight along the handguard's side and pointing forward (knuckle 25°, spread −16°) instead of curling over its top edge, where its tip stood in the red dot's window (the AK-47's lower seat does not transfer: the LSW-6's index goes into the handguard at any drop). `FpArms.try_hand` measures a candidate pose for one hand (each finger's depth and its points in weapon space) without a rebuild, and `FpArms.preview_hand` draws one in the game, for choosing such values: the LSW-6's was chosen by rendering every depth-clear candidate aimed through the red dot and counting the hand's pixels inside the window (1151 before, none after).

## Hit zones

`rust/sim/src/hitbox.rs` defines a player's hit zones: a head sphere and capsules for the neck, chest, stomach, hips, arms, and legs. Its inputs are everything a snapshot says about a body: feet position, yaw, pitch, stance (standing, crouching, sliding), activity (sprinting, in the air, mantling, reloading, aim, and the weapon in hand), and how far along its animations it is (stride phase, reload progress, mantle progress). What you see is what you hit: the zones follow the drawn soldier in every state another player can be seen in.

- **Base poses.** Each state starts from the skeleton of the animation the character plays in it, recorded in `rust/sim/src/hitbox/poses.rs`. Still poses (`Idle`, `Crouch_Idle`, `Slide`, `Jump`) are averaged over their clips. The standing blend is measured at 16 stride phases at each speed a player moves at (the walk, an aimed walk, the jog, the 4 m/s run every non-sprinting player settles at, and the sprint), the crouched walk at 16 phases, and a mantle's climb and a vault at 17 points each; the zones pick the measured poses around the snapshot's phase and blend them (`rust/sim/src/hitbox/gait.rs`, shared with `SoldierRig`).
- **Animation progress.** The server keeps each player's stride phase, reload progress, and mantle progress (`hitbox::clock::BodyClock`) and sends them in every snapshot (a byte each). Every loop runs on that one phase: their timelines are stretched to a second and offset so the left foot is furthest forward at phase 0, and `SoldierRig` seeks them to it rather than letting them run free. The climb (or the vault, `PlayerView::vaulting`) is seeked to the mantle's progress and blends in over its first 15%, the same share the zones blend it in; dropping off the far side of a vault holds the vault's last pose until the landing, in both. So every client draws a body at the moment of each animation its hit zones are posed at, and the server's rewind poses the same moment. `godot/tests/hitbox_fit_test.gd` generates `poses.rs` (`HITBOX_FIT_DUMP=$PWD/rust/sim/src/hitbox/poses.rs`, then `cargo fmt`); regenerate it when an animation changes.
- **Turning on the spot.** Standing still, the legs and hips stay on the feet as the aim turns (`Progress::turn`), turned about the feet, and the walk poses blend in by `Progress::step` while the feet step round, as the character draws them.
- **Velocity.** Snapshots carry none, so a sample blended between two snapshots takes the velocity from one to the other (`RemoteSample::velocity`). The client's interpolation and the server's rewind blend the same two snapshots, so both get the same velocity. It picks the loops by speed and direction of travel as the tree does (the zones' poses are measured in every authored direction, and halfway between those behind the strafes and either side of forward, where the drawn blend of two quite different leg poses is far from a straight line, `gait::POSE_DIRECTIONS`; within the forward diagonals they are measured on up the sprint, `gait::SPRINT_POSE_DIRECTIONS`).
- **Aim.** The pitch bends the spine, neck, and head in the same shares the character's poser uses, so the head and chest lean as drawn.
- **Arms.** The weapon sits where the character holds it, by class: at the hip, aimed, sprinting, or along the class's reload path, from the same numbers (`rust/sim/src/hitbox/carry.rs`, which the poser and `SoldierRig` read too). The hands go on that weapon's grips (each weapon file's `grips`, matching its scene's `GripRight` and `GripLeft` markers) and two-bone IK places the elbows, as on the character.
- What the zones do not follow: the rig's smoothing between states (a fraction of a second), the climb fading out after a mantle ends (0.15 s), a knife swing (half a second; snapshots say who is swinging, not how far along), and the short hit flinch.

`hitbox_fit_test.gd` poses a remote player through every state (idle, walking, running, sprinting, strafing, backpedalling, the diagonals and directions between authored ones, crouched and crouch-walking in several directions, sliding, aimed, reloading, mantling, vaulting and dropping off the far side, in the air, each weapon, pitch from -89° to 89°, turning) and, per zone, measures how far the skeleton's bones are from the capsule axes and how far outside the zone's shapes the skinned mesh lies. In every state, moving, reloading, and climbing ones included, it fails when a zone's bones stray more than 6 cm from its axes or when one in ten of its vertices lies more than 5 cm outside its shapes; only a pump gun's reach for shells (8 cm, arm) and a mantle's or a vault's legs (9 cm) get a little more. It also checks that each weapon file's `grips` match its scene's markers. `capture_hitbox_fit.gd` captures the overlay in the same states.

| Zone | Damage |
| --- | --- |
| Head | the weapon's `headshot` multiplier (1.2 to 1.5) |
| Chest, stomach | 1.0 |
| Arms | 0.9 |
| Legs | 0.85 |

With these, the M40 still kills with one shot to the head, chest, or stomach, but not to a limb.

The hitboxes are a pure function of snapshot fields, not of the animated skeleton. The dedicated server never runs animation, and Phase 3's lag compensation has to rebuild the hitboxes a player had several ticks ago. A pure function makes both cheap and identical on every machine: the client's zones and the server's rewind call the same function on the same interpolated snapshot. The debug overlay shows the zones over the model.

- `HitboxRig` (`rust/game/src/hitbox_rig.rs`) turns the shapes into physics bodies on the `hitboxes` layer. Each body carries `hit_zone` metadata, so the player's hitscan gets the zone from whatever it hits.
- **F4** toggles the overlay in debug builds: every character's hitboxes drawn over it, coloured by zone. Release builds ignore the key, and the overlay is depth tested, so it never shows players through walls.
- Range dummies block movement with a capsule on the `bodies` layer (layer 5). Bullets ignore that layer and hit the zones.

## Weapon sights

![Aimed down the M4A1, AK-47, LSW-6, M870, M1911, PDW-9, and BR-3 sights](media/iron-sights.jpg)

The M4A1, AK-47, LSW-6, and M1911 aim through the source models' own sights, and the M870 over its receiver at its bead. Modelled at real size, some of those sights are too small to read at game scale: a real post is 2 mm wide, 3 px at half a metre on a 900-line screen. `tools/build_weapons.py` reshapes them in view space (`sight_edits`, `edit_sights`), and moves the M4A1, whose sights sit 0.8 mm left of the source's centre line, onto the line (`sight.x`):

| Weapon | Rear | Front |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M4A1 | Flip-up leaf, its 2.4 mm precision peep opened to the 4.6 mm close-range aperture | Post thickened from 1.5 to 2.4 mm |
| AK-47, LSW-6 | Tangent leaf, its U-notch opened from 2.8 to 4 mm | Post thickened from 1.7 to 3.4 mm, inside its hood |
| M870 | The receiver's top, 13 mm under the eye | A 5 mm brass bead on the barrel's end (`tools/weapon_m870.py`) |
| M1911 | Notched blade, as modelled | Post with a green tritium dot, as modelled |

On the AK pattern's irons (AK-47 and LSW-6) the eye sits 30 cm behind the rear notch, 16 cm further back than with an optic (`iron_eye_back` on the view scene), with the cheek on the stock behind the receiver. Nearer, the eye was over the dust cover, which ran 5 cm behind it, and the near plane sliced the cover open into a hollow sheet. Now the sight picture is the AK's: the cover's ribbed rear and its spring-guide button low in the frame, the receiver running up to the tangent sight, and the post in the notch. The body comes back with the eye (`FpArms::set_body_shift`), so the arms keep their reach and the firing elbow stays low, and the firing thumb lies along the receiver's right side under its edge (`hand_tuning`) instead of standing up beside the cover. The source paints the AK's charging handle and selector lever near-white and polished: the build box-projects both onto the dust cover's worn blued steel in the atlas (`refinish`) and thins the lever from 7.5 to 3 mm (`thin`). `weapon_models_test` checks that, aimed on irons, nothing of the weapon comes within 4 cm of the eye.

The PDW-9 and BR-3 carry a compact red dot (`IronSights` style `reflex`, `rust/game/src/sights.rs`): a hooded window, a coated lens, and a 0.6 mm red dot, about 6 px across aimed, so it stays crisp through the bloom. The same sight is the red dot attachment. Aimed, every reflex window sits about 10 cm from the eye, spanning about an eighth of the screen's width: the attachment slides along the rail from the weapon's `OpticMount` to sit there (`reflex_seat` in `viewmodel.rs`; the M4A1's moves 8 cm back over the receiver), and it stands on a 12 mm riser, a lower-third co-witness, so the front sight and the support hand sit low in the window.

Both optics' housings are modelled in Blender (`tools/weapon_optics.py`, built by `tools/build_weapons.sh acog reddot` into `assets/weapons/optics/`) and finished like the M4A1 (`tools/weapon_finish.py`): hard-anodised aluminium that wears on its edges, rubber, and steel bolts, on a 1024 px texture each. The red dot is a base plate and body, a raked hood round the window, the emitter, a button pod with two rubber buttons, elevation and windage screws, and a slotted cross bolt (3.1k triangles). The ACOG is a rubber eye guard, a tube to a prism housing with a raised rib, ribbed elevation and windage caps, an objective bell with a sunshade lip, "2.5X" engraved on its left, and a clamp mount with two cross bolts and thumb nuts (9.1k). Each also exports `World`, decimated to 30%, for other players. `IronSights` still builds the lens, the dot, and the chevron and its post in code, so they line up exactly, and adds the model's surfaces round them, scaled with the optic: the red dot's into its sight mesh, the ACOG's eye guard into `SightMesh` and the rest into `SightBody`. When the rail sits lower than a housing's foot (the BR-3's), a riser fills the gap.

The ACOG (`acog` in `rust/game/src/sights.rs`) is a magnified prism sight in the manner of modern Call of Duty optics, not a scope overlay. Aimed, the whole view zooms (the weapon's `ads_fov_scale`) and the eye sits 4.5 cm behind the eyepiece (`ACOG_EYE_RELIEF`), so its rim frames a clear picture about half the screen tall with a lit chevron on a thin post and three drop marks. The housing ahead of the eyepiece is its own mesh (`SightBody`), hidden from 90% aimed, when it is behind the rim or inside the picture anyway, and any weapon or hand fragment inside the lens past the reticle is cut away (`viewmodel_behind_lens` and the `viewmodel_scope_lens` global, set each frame by `ViewmodelRig`), so the eye sees only the zoomed world and the reticle. The cut lives in copies of `viewmodel.gdshader` and `character.gdshader` built at runtime (`lens_clipped_shader` in `viewmodel.rs`), which the local player's weapon and arms switch to only while an ACOG is fitted; every other draw keeps a shader without `discard`, and with it the cheap depth prepass. It stands 1.2 cm higher than a red dot (`ACOG_RISER`), as on the real rifle, and an aimed shot shoves it only a third as far toward the eye. The M40 keeps its full scope overlay, but raising it reads as bringing the scope to the eye. From half aimed, the same cut opens behind the scope's eyepiece (`scope_eyepiece` on the view scene), so the zoomed world shows in its glass instead of a black disc. From 60% the cut widens from the glass to the overlay's lens, which follows the eyepiece on screen, while the overlay's black surround and then its reticle fade in (`scope_open`; `scripts/scope_overlay.gd`). At 85% the weapon hides and the overlay is complete, so nothing cuts in.

Rules the sights follow:

- **Sight line.** Each weapon scene's origin is on the sight line, so at full ADS the sight line is the camera's axis. An aperture is centred on it. A notch sight follows "equal height, equal light": the front post's tip is level with the top of the rear blade, and the post sits in the middle of the notch with light either side of it.
- **Readable at game scale.** Posts are 2.4 mm or more, so they show at least 4 px wide aimed; peeps and notches open enough to frame them.
- **Clear picture.** Nothing in front of the eye covers the front sight. The M870 sits 25 cm out when aimed, its firing hand low on the wrist, so neither the receiver nor the thumb over the wrist rises into the picture; held closer, the thumb came within a centimetre of the eye and the near plane cut it into slivers. Its support hand holds the back half of the pump, within reach of a bent arm.

`godot/tests/weapon_models_test.gd` traces the picture from where the eye is when aimed: the line just over each front sight must run clear through the rear sight, and a millimetre under its tip, on the line and 0.6 mm either side, the first thing the eye meets must be the front sight itself.

To review sights, capture every weapon at the hip and aimed: `CAPTURE_DIR=/path/to/out godot --path godot --resolution 1600x900 --script res://tests/capture_sights.gd` (needs a GPU and a display, or Xvfb). `LOOK=0.6,0` aims at a bare wall instead of the range's targets, and `WEAPONS=m4,m870` captures only some.

## Zombies and other characters

Zombies use the same skeleton and pipeline. Five zombie kits in `tools/build_character_assets.py` are five different people in their own clothes: a young man in a sweater and cargo trousers (`zombie`), a heavy middle-aged man in work overalls (`zombie_b`), an old man in a jacket and jeans (`zombie_c`), a slim young man in a T-shirt and jeans (`zombie_d`), and a woman in a blouse, a pencil skirt, and ankle boots (`zombie_e`). Each has its own MakeHuman body, skin, and atlas (2048 for `zombie`, 1024 for the others, which are seen less and never close for long), with bare hands and no kit. Each has short hair and stubble painted onto the head (`paint_hair` in `tools/blender_characters.py`; soldiers get the same, a close crop under the helmet and stubble on bare faces): a hairline mask over the scalp, sideburns, and the jaw, which the skin bake turns into strands of the kit's hair colour with a little relief. MakeHuman's own hair is alpha-tested cards, which the one opaque character material cannot draw. Zombie kits are not tinted: their clothes keep the garments' own colours (`Kit::Zombie` in `palette.rs`), and the shader makes them filthier and darker than the garments' own textures: bright fabric is pressed down toward a worn value (`1 / (1 + 3l)`, so a white T-shirt or pale overalls read soiled grey instead of glowing under the floodlights), then ground-in dirt is worked in, heaviest as mud up the legs, by a per-zombie amount. Skin is drained to a sallow pallor: its values are pressed together (`0.3 (l / 0.4)^0.6`), so a dark-skinned face is lifted out of black at 1 m while painted hair, stubble, and wounds stay darker than the skin round them, then tinted sickly and mottled.

Each zombie body's decay is painted into its atlas (`add_decay` in `tools/blender_characters.py`, from `decay` in each zombie kit), in the rest pose's object space, so it lands on the same place on skin and clothes alike:

- **Wounds** (each a point and a radius): torn flesh with a dark crust on skin. Blood soaks out round each wound in a ragged stain broken up at two scales, browner and dry at its edge and darker and wetter near the wound (lower roughness), and runs down the body below it in streaks.
- **A bloody mouth and chin**, placed from the eyes so it finds each head's mouth.
- **Rips** in the cloth: dark slits with a lighter frayed lip.
- **Grime:** the clothes yellowed, greyed, and dulled, then blotched, with mud rising from the ground to the knees. On skin, bruising and faint veins.

Wounds also cover the MakeHuman logo printed on the T-shirt. The skin kept round a neckline is trimmed to what the clothes leave uncovered (`uncovered` in `keep_body`), so it meets the collar with no gap. The shader's own noise blood is gone: it splashed bright red blobs on heads at random.

`scenes/characters/zombie_body.tscn` lists the other bodies in `SoldierRig.variants`, and each zombie wears one of the four, picked from its seed. `SoldierRig` plays the Universal Animation Library 2 zombie clips when `zombie` is set and adds glowing eyes. A horde does not walk in step: each zombie starts its walk, jog, and run loops at its own moment of the stride (a phase from its seed), and walks up to 8% faster or slower than its round's speed (`SPEED_JITTER` in `zombie.rs`, spread evenly so the round's average holds), with its cadence following its speed so the feet do not slide. `SoldierRig.body` forces one body (captures use it to show each kit). To add another character, add a kit to `KITS` (its MakeHuman body, clothes, and gear), and either a scene that instances its model under a `SoldierRig` or an entry in a rig's `variants`.

### Minimap

The minimap follows Black Ops 2's and every Call of Duty since: a square window, top left, centred on you, turning with you so you always face up. Settings, General, "Rotate minimap" turns that off and keeps north up instead, as Modern Warfare 2019's option does. It shows 64 metres across. You are a white arrow in the middle, teammates are blue arrows pointing where they look, and the mode's objectives sit on it in their holder's colour: Capture the Flag's flags, the live Hardpoint, Domination's lettered flags, and Search and Destroy's sites and dropped or planted charge. An objective out of the window is held at its edge. Enemies show only as red dots, and only where the server reveals them: firing a weapon without a suppressor puts the shooter on every enemy's minimap where they fired, for 3 seconds, fading over the last one (`rust/sim/src/radar.rs`; Call of Duty gives no number, and players measure "a few seconds"). The dot stays where the shot was fired, a new shot moves it, and it goes when the shooter dies. A suppressor keeps you off it; knives and grenades show nothing. The server sends each player only the dots their own radar shows (`RadarView` in each snapshot). It hides in killcams and in Zombies, which has none in Call of Duty either.

![The minimap on Warehouse in Domination, with the score block under it](media/minimap.jpg)

Each map's plan is a top-down image rendered from its collision by `godot/tools/build_minimaps.gd` (`godot --headless --path godot --script res://tools/build_minimaps.gd`, or `MINIMAPS=slamcity` for one map). It casts rays down through every pixel: floors are graphite, lighter the higher they stand; walls and solid cover are pale, cut at 2.1 metres above the floor so building interiors show, as Call of Duty's do; floors under a roof are a shade darker, so buildings read as blocks; and a pale line marks every step taller than a kerb. Our plans are flat and colourless where Black Ops 2 painted its from the level's own textures, to keep the HUD quiet. Run the tool again after a map's layout changes. The images are in `godot/assets/minimaps/`, and each map's square in metres is in `godot/scripts/net/minimap_data.gd`.

Weapon silhouettes in `assets/ui/weapons/` are rendered from the weapon models by `godot/tools/weapon_icons.gd`; run it again after a model changes.
